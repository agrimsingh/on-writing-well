# Clutter and Usage: Lookup Tables

Quick-reference tables for the word and clutter passes. Entries marked `*` are examples Zinsser uses in *On Writing Well*. Unmarked entries are modern additions in the same spirit, covering the clutter that shows up in today's docs, tickets, emails and corporate prose.

These are prompts, not laws. Zinsser's test for any word or phrase: **"Do we need it?"** Usage is relative and changes week to week. Be **liberal in accepting new words that bring strength or color, and conservative in grammar.** Choose the grammatical form unless it sounds affected (Marianne Moore).

### Adjectives by habit (Zinsser's examples)
stately elms\*, frisky kittens\*, hard-bitten detectives\*, sleepy lagoons\*, gnarled oak\*. An adjective earns its place only when it does work the noun can't: "garish daffodils" (a judgment), "red dirt" (when the dirt really is red).

### Slippery intensifiers to retire
decidedly\*, eminently (fair)\*, arguably (the best)\*, virtually\*, admittedly\*, totally, absolutely, incredibly, literally (when not literal), truly, really, extremely, highly, deeply, utterly. If it can be proved, drop "arguably". If it's open to argument, say so.

## Little qualifiers

Prune them. Each one whittles away the reader's trust. **Don't be kind of bold. Be bold.**

a bit\*, a little\*, sort of\*, kind of\*, rather\*, quite\*, very\*, too\* (as "very")\*, pretty much\*, pretty (as "very")\*, in a sense\*, somewhat\*, fairly, slightly, mostly, more or less, to some extent, in some ways, relatively, perhaps, possibly, it seems, it appears, I think (when the opinion is obviously yours), I feel, I believe, basically, essentially, generally, just, simply, actually.

- "Weren't too happy because the hotel was pretty expensive"\* → "weren't happy because the hotel was expensive".
- "A bit confused, sort of tired, a little depressed and somewhat annoyed"\* → confused, tired, depressed, annoyed.
- "Too" as "very" is clutter ("He didn't feel too much like going shopping"\*). In a wry sense it's fine ("He was not too happy when she ignored him"\*).
- "Rather unique"\* is as possible as "rather pregnant". Unique, perfect, dead, complete and infinite don't take degrees.

## Throat-clearing and announcing

Phrases that explain how you're going to explain. **If you might add, add it. If it should be pointed out, point it out. If it's interesting to note, make it interesting.**

| Cut | Notes |
|---|---|
| I might add*, it should be pointed out*, it is interesting to note* | Just say it. |
| this will interest you* | Readers stop being interested. |
| it is important to note that, it is worth noting that, it should be noted that | Just say it. |
| needless to say | Then don't say it. |
| it goes without saying | Then let it go. |
| as we all know, as everyone knows | If they know, why tell them? |
| in this article / document / section I will | Start doing it. |
| the purpose of this memo is to | State the purpose as the first sentence. |
| let me be clear, to be honest, frankly | Implies you weren't before. |
| in a sense*, in a very real sense | Means nothing. |
| in today's fast-paced world, in this day and age | Stock opener; cut. |
| in sum*, in conclusion, to summarize, in summary | The sound of cranking; stop instead. |
| what insights can we glean*… | Same. |
| it remains to be seen, it is too early to tell | Take a stand or cut. |
| at the end of the day | Cut. |
| I hope this email finds you well | Optional courtesy at most; start with the point. |

## Clichés

**Clichés are the enemy of taste and the kiss of death.** Even careful writers use them in a first draft. Find them by reading aloud, then replace them with your own fresh phrase or a plain statement.

From Zinsser's Serpico example: shouldered his way\*, only to be met\*, crashing into his face\*, waging a lonely war\*, corruption that is rife\*, sending shock waves\*, New York's finest\*. Also in the book: enjoyed a spell of illness\*, enjoying a slump\*, the kiss of death\*, in the final analysis\*, leaves no stone unturned\*, tried and true\*, the same old chestnuts\*.

More to catch: at the end of the day, think outside the box, level playing field, paradigm shift, perfect storm, game changer, raise the bar, hit the ground running, tip of the iceberg, food for thought, the elephant in the room, few and far between, last but not least, in the nick of time, a whole new ballgame, avoid like the plague, crystal clear, push the envelope, best of both worlds, by leaps and bounds, it's not rocket science, at first glance, needless to say, a double-edged sword, time will tell, only time will tell, the calm before the storm.

## Travelese

Syrupy words and platitudes that sound like a brochure.

wondrous\*, dappled\*, roseate\*, fabled\*, scudding\*, quaint\* (windmills, covered bridges), nestled\* (hill towns), half-forgotten\* (byways), old meets new\*, smiling storefronts\*, beckoning ruins\*, attractive\*, charming\*, romantic\*, hidden gem, breathtaking, picturesque, bustling, vibrant (as filler), unspoiled, off the beaten path, a feast for the senses, a melting pot, a land of contrasts.

The fix: **find the detail that is unique to this place**, and write as a person, not a brochure.

## Sports English

southpaw\*, circuit clout\*, twin killing\*, hurler\*, pigskin\*, hoopsters\*, grapplers\*, a set as a "stanza"\*. Write good English and **repeat the clearest noun** (the batter, the runner). Don't disguise one person as "the gangling junior", "the Memphis native" and "the redhead"\*.

The same applies to any field's private slang. Don't write a technical piece in "dev-speak" or a finance piece in "Street-speak" when plain English will do.

| impacted with the ground prematurely* | crashed |

| a negative cash-flow position* | broke |

| at the vortex of cruciality* | crucial |

Orwell: political language consists largely of **"euphemism, question-begging and sheer cloudy vagueness."** In business writing the fix is to **locate the missing "I"** and say who did what.

## Concept nouns and creeping nounism

Concept nouns hide the people and the verb. Turn them back into people doing things.

| Dead | Alive |
|---|---|
| The common reaction is incredulous laughter.* | Most people just laugh with disbelief. |
| Bemused cynicism isn't the only response to the old system.* | Some people respond to the old system by turning cynical; others say… |
| The current campus hostility is a symptom of the change.* | It's easy to notice the change: you can see how angry the students are. |
| money problem areas* | (they're going broke) |
| precipitation activity*, thunderstorm probability situation* | it may rain |
| communication facilitation skills development intervention* | a program to help students write better |
| Evaluative procedures for the objectives were also established based on acceptable criteria.* | At the end of the year we will evaluate our progress. |
| pre-feasibility studies are in the paperwork stage* | we're starting to look into whether it can be done |
| the implementation of the migration was completed | we finished the migration |
| perform an analysis of | analyze |
| provide a recommendation | recommend |
| make an improvement to | improve |
| have a discussion about | discuss |
| there was a failure of the deployment | the deployment failed |
| user authentication verification process | checking that users are who they say they are (or: login) |

Look for words ending in **-tion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ness, -ization** next to weak verbs (is, was, have, make, give, provide, perform, conduct). Each one is a candidate for a real verb.

## Pre-labeling and overstatement

**Trust your material.** Let facts surprise on their own.

- **Cut the pre-labels:** surprisingly\*, predictably\*, of course\*, interestingly, ironically (unless it truly is), remarkably, amazingly, shockingly, sadly, needless to say.
- **Cut the exclamation point** that announces a surprise or flags a joke.
- **Overstatement**\*: "as if an atomic bomb had gone off there"\*, "ten 747 jets flying through my brain"\*. Let the humor sneak up.
- **Credibility:** one inflated claim makes everything else suspect. Replace "massive", "huge", "game-changing" and "unprecedented" with the number.

## Usage distinctions

Classic distinctions the usage panel upheld. **Incorrect usage loses you the readers you'd most like to win.**

| Pair | Distinction |
|---|---|
| can / may* | *Can* is ability; *may* is permission or possibility. |
| fewer / less* | *Fewer* for things you count (fewer bugs); *less* for amounts (less time). |
| eldest / oldest* | *Eldest* for members of a family; *oldest* generally. |
| flout / flaunt* | *Flout* is to defy a rule; *flaunt* is to show off. |
| fortuitous / fortunate* | *Fortuitous* means accidental, not lucky. |
| disinterested / uninterested* | *Disinterested* means impartial; *uninterested* means not interested. |
| infer / imply* | The speaker *implies*; the listener *infers*. |
| reference / allusion* | A *reference* names something directly; an *allusion* hints at it. |
| connive / conspire* | *Connive* is to wink at wrongdoing (tacit consent); *conspire* is to plot together. |
| compare with / compare to* | *Compare with* examines likenesses and differences; *compare to* likens one thing to another. |
| comprise* | Means *include* or *consist of*. "Dinner comprises meat, potatoes, salad and dessert."* The whole comprises the parts; the parts compose the whole. Not "is comprised of". |
| that / which* | Default to *that* (restrictive). Use *, which* for a nonrestrictive clause that describes, locates or explains: "Take the shoes, which are in the closet."* |
| myself* | Not a substitute for *me* ("invited Mary and myself"*, rejected by 95%). "'Myself' is the refuge of idiots taught early that 'me' is a dirty word." (Red Smith) |
| -wise* | "Healthwise" is doltwise. Cut the suffix. |
| rather unique* | Unique takes no degrees. |
| too / very* | *Too* for *very* is clutter, except in wry use. |
| cohort / colleague* | *Cohort* for colleague only in jocular use (at the reunion, in funny hats). |
| like (as conjunction)* | Prefer *as* in writing ("as many people do"). |
| contact (verb)* | Rejected by 66% of the panel. Prefer *call*, *write*, *meet*. |
| to author* | "Good God, no! Never!" (Barbara Tuchman). Use *write*. |
| good (adverb)* | "The exclusive property of Ernest Hemingway." Use *well*. |
| regime, dynasty* | Technical terms (Barzun). Use *administration*. |
| presently* | Means *soon*, not *now*. |
| affect / effect | *Affect* (verb) is to influence; *effect* (noun) is result; *effect* (verb) is to bring about. |
| its / it's | *Its* is possessive; *it's* is *it is*. |
| who / whom | *Who* is subject; *whom* is object. Recast if it sounds stiff. |
| e.g. / i.e. | *e.g.* means for example; *i.e.* means that is. |
| principal / principle | *Principal* is chief (or a school head); *principle* is a rule. |
| ensure / insure / assure | *Ensure* is to make certain; *insure* is to cover with insurance; *assure* is to tell confidently. |
| historic / historical | *Historic* is important in history; *historical* concerns history. |
| continual / continuous | *Continual* recurs; *continuous* is unbroken. |
| literally | Means actually, not figuratively. |

Settled in favor of the speaker's comfortable way: **split infinitives** (half the panel objected) and **ending with a preposition** ("a sentence is a fine thing to put a preposition at the end of"\*). The spoken language is looser than the written, and "the pen must at length comply with the tongue" (Samuel Johnson).

## Words Zinsser accepts vs. rejects

| Accepts (good, vivid, needed) | Rejects (cheap, pompous, unneeded) |
|---|---|
| hassle\*, freak\* (enthusiast), dropout\* (97%), escalate\*, rambunctious\*, trigger\*, rile\*, shambles\*, tycoon\*, trek\*, bottom line\*, launder\*, stonewall\*, printout\*, O.K.\*, cyberspace\*, meltdown\*, software\*, fax\*, wetlands\*, gentrify\*, rip-off\*, vibes\* | notables\*, greats\*, upcoming\*, finalize\*, to author\*, prioritize\*, disincentive\*, input\* (for opinion), feedback\*, interface\*, dialogue\* (verb), paradigm\*, parameter\*, potentialize\*, senior citizen\*, definitize\*, -wise\*, myself\* (for me), ongoing\*, impact\* (verb) |

The writer who cares about usage "must always know the quick from the dead". Slang that arrives overnight often leaves just as fast ("happenings", "out of sight").

## Nonsexist alternatives

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| gal\*, the girls\* (adults) | women, (their names) |
| poetess\*, authoress, lady lawyer\* | poet, author, lawyer |
| housewife\* (when not relevant) | (omit, or homemaker if relevant) |
| coed\*, divorcée\*, blonde\* (as noun), "shapely stewardess"\*, "pert brunette"\* | student, (the person's name or role) |
| early settlers pushed west with their wives and children\* | pioneer families; men and women who settled the West |
| chairman (generic) | chair, head; or recast ("Speaking for the company, Ms. Jones said…"\*) |
| spokesman | spokesperson, representative, or recast |
| actors and actresses | performers\* |
| mankind | people, humanity |
| manpower | staff, workforce, people |
| man-hours | work hours, hours |
| each employee should decide what he thinks | employees should decide what they think; **you**; **we**; or drop the pronoun |
| he/she\* | **never**. Recast, or use "he or she" sparingly |

Best approach: **change other parts of the sentence** so the pronoun disappears. Use "we", "you", "our" or "the" instead of "his", or a plural general noun ("Doctors often neglect their families"\*). Avoid converting every "he" into "they" as a reflex; it turns the style to mush. Modern style guides now accept singular "they" for an unknown or nonbinary person; use it where it reads naturally.

## Punctuation and mechanics quick table

| Mark | Use |
|---|---|
| Period | Reach it sooner. Split two thoughts into two sentences. |
| Exclamation point | Almost never. Never to flag a joke. |
| Semicolon | Sparingly; it slows the pace. Prefer the period or the dash. |
| Dash | Single: amplifies or justifies. Pair: sets off an aside. |
| Colon | A brief halt before a list or example. |
| Parentheses | Asides that are truly parenthetical; don't hide the point in them. |
| Quotation marks | For quotations. Not for "emphasis" or to apologize for a word ("I'm mucking about in the argot"*). If you use the word, own it. |
| Contractions | Use them (I'll, won't, can't) for warmth. Avoid I'd/he'd (had or would?). Don't invent them (could've*). |
| "But" to open a sentence | Encouraged. The strongest way to signal contrast. |
| "However" | Early in the sentence, not first, never last. |
| "There is / There are" opener | Usually weak. Lead with the real subject. |
| Numbers | Report the ones that matter. Don't dump statistics. |
