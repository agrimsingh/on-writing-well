## The Transaction

- **No single right method.** Day or night, silence or radio, by hand or keyboard, one fast draft then revision or fiddling endlessly with paragraph one. Whatever helps you say what you want is the right method.
- **Every writer is tense.** People sit down to "commit an act of literature", and the self that appears on the page is stiffer than the person who sat down. The job is to find the real person behind the tension.
- **The product is who you are.** What holds readers on a subject they never cared about is the writer's enthusiasm: how they were drawn in, what they brought, how it changed them.
- **Humanity and warmth** are the two qualities to pursue. Good writing has an aliveness that keeps readers going from paragraph to paragraph. That comes from using English with the greatest clarity and strength, not from gimmicks to "personalize" the author.
- **Writing is a craft, not an art.** Professionals keep a daily schedule and don't wait for inspiration. If writing is your job, you learn to do it on bad days too.
- **Rewriting is the essence of writing.** Professionals rewrite, then rewrite what they rewrote.
- The principles may not be teachable, but most of them can be learned.

## Simplicity

- **Clutter is the disease of modern writing**: unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills, meaningless jargon. The urge is to inflate and sound important. A pilot "presently anticipating experiencing considerable precipitation" means *it may rain*.
- **Strip every sentence to its cleanest components.** The adulterants are every word with no function, every long word that could be short, every adverb that repeats the verb, and every passive that leaves the reader unsure who is doing what. They tend to grow in proportion to education and rank.
- **Model:** Franklin Roosevelt turned a bureaucratic blackout order (preparations to "completely obscure all Federal buildings… from visibility by reason of internal or external illumination") into plain instructions: in buildings where work must go on, *put something across the windows*.
- **Simplify, simplify** (Thoreau). Thoreau says plainly and in order what is on his mind.
- **Clear your head of clutter first.** Clear thinking becomes clear writing; neither exists without the other. A muddy thinker may get away with a paragraph or two, but the reader will soon be lost, and lost readers rarely come back.
- **The reader** has an attention span of about 30 seconds and endless competitors, sleep the strongest. A reader dozing over an article was given too much unnecessary trouble.
- **A lost reader is the writer's fault.** Common causes:
  - a sentence so cluttered that its meaning disappears;
  - a sentence built so shoddily it can be read several ways;
  - a pronoun or tense switched mid-sentence, so the reader loses track of who is talking or when;
  - sentence B not following logically from sentence A, because the link was clear only in the writer's head;
  - a word misused because the writer didn't look it up ("sanguine" vs. "sanguinary", "infer" vs. "imply").
- Readers are tenacious at first and blame themselves. They reread and guess like someone deciphering an ancient rune. Then they quit and look for a better writer.
- **The two questions.** *What am I trying to say?* (surprisingly often the writer doesn't know). Then: *Have I said it? Is it clear to someone encountering the subject for the first time?* If not, fuzz has crept into the machinery. A clear writer is clearheaded enough to see fuzz for what it is.
- **Clear thinking is a conscious act** you force on yourself, like making a shopping list or doing an algebra problem. Nobody is born clearheaded.
- **Writing is hard work.** A clear sentence is no accident. Remember this in moments of despair. If you find it hard, that's because it is hard.
- Zinsser rewrote nearly every page of the book four or five times, each time tighter, stronger and more precise, then read it aloud and still found clutter to cut.

## Clutter

- **Clutter is like weeds**: new kinds sprout overnight and the writer is always slightly behind ("at this point in time" for *now*).
- **Prepositions draped on verbs:** head *up*, face *up to*, free *up*, order *up*. Not a small detail: writing improves in direct ratio to the number of things kept out that shouldn't be there.
- **Redundant modifiers.** "Personal" friend, "his personal feeling", "her personal physician". "His" already makes the feeling his. Friends are friends; doctors are doctors.
- **Laborious phrases displacing short words.** "Currently", "at the present time" and "presently" (which means *soon*) for *now*. Use "now" for the immediate moment, "today" for the historical present, or just the verb ("It is raining").
- **"Experiencing"** is one of the ultimate clutterers. The dentist asks if you're "experiencing any pain" but would ask his own child "Does it hurt?" Pompous professional language makes the speaker sound important and blunts the edge of truth.
- **Euphemism.** A slum becomes a "depressed socioeconomic area", garbage collectors become "waste-disposal personnel", the dump becomes a "volume reduction unit". This is political correctness gone amok.
- **Concealment.** Corporations and governments hide mistakes in fog: layoffs as "involuntary methodologies", a crash as "impacted with the ground prematurely", a plant closing as a "volume-related production-schedule adjustment", going broke as "a negative cash-flow position", an invasion as a "reinforced protective reaction strike". Orwell warned that political language defends the indefensible through euphemism, question-begging and cloudy vagueness. Clutter is not just a nuisance; it can be a deadly tool.
- **Long words no better than short ones:** assistance (help), numerous (many), facilitate (ease), individual (man or woman), remainder (rest), initial (first), implement (do), sufficient (enough), attempt (try), referred to as (called). The full list is in [clutter-and-usage.md](clutter-and-usage.md).
- **Fad words:** paradigm, parameter, prioritize, potentialize. Don't "dialogue" with someone you can talk to. Don't "interface" with anybody.
- **Explaining how you'll explain:** "I might add", "It should be pointed out", "It is interesting to note". If you might add it, add it. If it should be pointed out, point it out. If it's interesting, make it interesting. ("This will interest you" stupefies everyone.)
- **Inflated phrases:** with the possible exception of (except), due to the fact that (because), he totally lacked the ability to (he couldn't), until such time as (until), for the purpose of (for).
- **The bracket method.** Put brackets around every component that isn't doing useful work:
  - the preposition appended to a verb ("order up");
  - the adverb that repeats the verb ("smile happily");
  - the adjective stating a known fact ("tall skyscraper");
  - the little qualifiers ("a bit", "sort of");
  - phrases that mean nothing ("in a sense");
  - whole sentences that repeat the previous one, say what readers don't need to know, or say what they can figure out themselves.
  
  **Most first drafts can be cut by 50 percent without losing information or the author's voice.** Brackets rather than deletion respect the writer: *"I may be wrong, but I think this can go without changing the meaning. You decide."* Students learned to bracket their own clutter mentally, and the brackets followed them for life.
- **Review questions for every sentence:** Is every word doing new work? Can any thought be expressed with more economy? Is anything pompous, pretentious or faddish? Am I hanging on to something useless because I think it's beautiful? Be grateful for everything you can throw away.

## Style

- **"If I cut all the clutter, will there be anything left of me?"** It's a fair question. Answer it first at the level of carpentry, then at the level of identity.
- **Few people realize how badly they write.** Told to cut eight pages to four, writers howl, then do it, and it's much better. Then comes the hard part: cutting to three.
- **Strip down before building up.** Learn to saw wood neatly and drive nails before beveling edges or adding finials. If the nails are weak, the house collapses. If the verbs are weak and the syntax rickety, the sentences fall apart.
- Writers like Tom Wolfe and Norman Mailer built ornate houses, but only after years of learning the craft. Nobody becomes Tom Wolfe overnight, not even Tom Wolfe. First learn to hammer nails, and take satisfaction in plain strength.
- **There is no style store.** Gaudy similes and tinseled adjectives draped on to look special are like a toupee: fine at first glance, but at second glance the man doesn't look like himself. Readers notice when you put on airs, and they want the person talking to sound genuine.
- **Fundamental rule: be yourself.** It's the hardest rule, because it requires two things writers find almost impossible: to relax and to have confidence.
- **Writers avoid writing** (trips to the water cooler far exceed bodily need). There's no cure. Some days go badly, and every writer has them.
- **Why the first paragraphs are bad.** The writer imagines the length, the august look in print, the readership, the need for authority and dazzle, and tightens up. Paragraph 1 is a tissue of machine-made generalities. By paragraph 3 or 4 a human voice emerges. Editors routinely throw away the first three or four paragraphs (or pages) and start where the writer begins to sound like a person. Watch for the sentence that signals a person, like "I'll never forget the day when I…"
- **Write in the first person.** Writing is an intimate transaction between two people. "Who am I to say what I think?" Who are you *not* to? Nobody else thinks or feels exactly as you do. They'll care if you tell them something interesting in words that come naturally.
- **Avoid the professorial "one"** ("One finds oneself not wholly in accord…") and the impersonal "it is to be hoped". "One" is a boring guy. Readers want a professor with a passion for the subject explaining why it fascinates them.
- **Where "I" isn't allowed** (news stories, many magazines, institutional reports, term papers), some of the bans are valid: news should be objective, and students shouldn't escape into opinion before doing the work. "I" can be self-indulgent. But you can still convey *I-ness*: good writers are visible just behind their words. If "I" is forbidden, think "I" while writing, or draft in first person and take the "I"s out afterward. It warms an impersonal style.
- Institutions sound alike because their people disappear behind impersonal passives ("initiatives were undertaken", "priorities have been identified").
- **Don't bob and weave.** Public figures hedge to avoid commitment ("increasingly brighter clouds"; a 13-word sentence with five hedging words: "And yet, on balance, affirmative action has, I think, been a qualified success"). Leaders who bob and weave don't inspire confidence, and neither do writers.
- **Sell yourself, and your subject will exert its own appeal.** Believe in your identity and opinions. Writing is an act of ego; admit it, and use its energy to keep going.

## The Audience

- **Write for yourself.** There's no mass audience; every reader is a different person. Don't guess what editors want or what the country is in the mood for. Editors and readers don't know what they want until they read it, and they always want something new.
- **Humor:** if it amuses you while writing, put it in. It can always come out later, but only you can put it in.
- **Resolving the paradox** (the impatient reader vs. writing for yourself). These are separate issues:
  - *Craft:* there's no excuse for losing readers through sloppy workmanship. Master the tools: simplify, prune, strive for order. Treat it as almost mechanical work.
  - *Attitude:* don't give a moment's worry to whether the reader likes you, your opinions, your humor or your vision of life. You'll get along or you won't.
- **Style emerges** from under the cleared clutter, growing more distinctive over the years, and keeps changing as you age. Some old writers keep their zest; some students write like desiccated alumni.
- **Never say anything in writing that you wouldn't comfortably say in conversation.** If you don't say "indeed" or "moreover", or call someone "a fine individual", don't write it.
- **Models of writing to please oneself:**
  - *E. B. White on the hen:* a subject the reader has no interest in, made delightful through simple rhythms, unexpected but fitting words ("deified", "allure", "cackling"), specific details (the Laced Wyandotte, the brooder house) and an unabashed decades-long devotion. Three paragraphs reveal the man.
  - *H. L. Mencken on the Scopes trial:* the opposite style, opulent and surging, but the same firm opinions. He wrote for himself, never kowtowed or curried favor, and you needn't share his prejudices to enjoy them. Courage makes influential writers.
  - *James Herndon on teaching:* a seemingly artless style full of art ("ain't" and "tendentious" in one sentence, quotes without quotation marks). It avoids the pretension of worthy writing and makes room for humor and common sense. A true voice, written for an audience of one.
- Zinsser kept "Who am I writing for?" instead of "whom". It just wasn't him.

## Words

- **Journalese** kills freshness. It's a quilt of instant words: adjectives used as nouns ("greats", "notables"), nouns used as verbs ("to host"), nouns chopped into verbs ("enthuse", "emote") or padded into verbs ("beef up", "put teeth into"), plus "famed", "staffers", "upcoming" and notes that are always "fired off". Fight these phrases or you'll sound like every hack.
- **The cliché-chain story.** A news piece built from "shouldered his way", "only to be met by", "crashing into his face", "waging a lonely war", "corruption that is rife", "sending shock waves" and "New York's finest" is banal: no surprise, no unusual word, no oblique look. Readers know they're with a hack and stop. Clichés also breed lazy logic: an "upcoming report" whose impact "has yet to be felt", fragments that "buzz".
- **Care deeply about words.** If you write that someone "enjoyed" a spell of illness or a business "enjoyed" a slump, ask how much they enjoyed it. Notice other writers' word choices and be finicky. *The race in writing is not to the swift but to the original.*
- **Learn by imitation.** Read the best of what's written today and by earlier masters, and work out how they did it. Cultivate the best models. Don't assume something is good because it was published; editing is often sloppy.
- **Use dictionaries.** Look up any word you're unsure of. Learn its etymology and unexpected meanings. Master the small differences between near-synonyms ("cajole", "wheedle", "blandish", "coax"). Keep a dictionary of synonyms.
- **Use a thesaurus** to jog memory, as a rhyming dictionary reminds a songwriter of the choices. Then go to the dictionary to learn how the candidates differ.
- **Sound matters.** Readers hear what they read with their inner ear, so rhythm and alliteration are vital in every sentence. Rearrange "These are the times that try men's souls" and you get oatmeal ("Soulwise, these are trying times"). Prose writers must be part poet, always listening. Choose words for emotional weight and sound ("serene" is soft; "tranquil" is oddly disturbing). Build sentences to end on a phrase that lingers.
- **Cure a plodding gait by ear.** If every sentence moves at the same pace, read them aloud. Reverse a sentence's order, substitute a fresh or odd word, vary sentence lengths. An occasional short sentence carries a tremendous punch and stays in the reader's ear.
- Read everything aloud before letting it go. Words are the only tools you have, and somebody out there is listening.

## Usage

- **Usage has no fixed boundaries.** Language changes weekly, and judgments are often a matter of taste. The goal is to keep the language from getting sloppy *and* to help it grow by welcoming useful newcomers.
- **Accept vivid, needed words:** "hassle", "freak" (enthusiast), "dropout", "escalate", "rambunctious", "trigger", "rile", "shambles", "tycoon", "trek" (any arduous trip), "launder" (money), "stonewall", "printout", "bottom line". Short countercultural words ("rip-off", "downer") won on brevity. But slang that arrives overnight often leaves overnight ("out of sight", "happenings"). Know the quick from the dead.
- **Reject cheap or pompous ones:** "notables", "greats", "upcoming", "to author", "-wise" suffixes ("healthwise"), "rather unique" (no more possible than rather pregnant), "senior citizen", "prioritize", "disincentive", "input/feedback" outside computing, "impact" as a verb, "ongoing", "definitize", "finalize". "Myself" as an object ("invited Mary and myself") is prissy; a usage panel rejected it 95 percent.
- **The pattern: be liberal in accepting new words, conservative in grammar.**
- **Test of convenience** (Theodore Bernstein): does the word fill a real need? If so, give it a franchise.
- **Speech is looser than print**, and print eventually follows speech. Split infinitives and sentence-ending prepositions are acceptable when the alternative is stiff.
- **Correctness can vary within one word.** "Cohort" for colleague is fine only jocularly. "Too" for "very" is clutter ("not too good") except when wry ("He was not too happy when she ignored him").
- **Keep the classic distinctions**, out of love for the language's precision. Incorrect usage loses you the readers you most want:
  - can / may; fewer / less; eldest / oldest
  - flout (defy) ≠ flaunt (show off)
  - fortuitous = accidental; disinterested = impartial
  - infer (reader deduces) ≠ imply (writer suggests)
  - reference ≠ allusion; connive ≠ conspire; compare with ≠ compare to
  - comprise = include ("dinner comprises meat, potatoes, salad and dessert"); never "comprised of"
- **"I choose always the grammatical form unless it sounds affected"** (Marianne Moore). Not pedantry, but no welcome mat for every atrocity.
- **Separate usage from jargon.** Jargon is a pompous coinage that sounds more important than an existing plain word ("prioritize" for *rank*). Usage is a new term that conveys a picturable image or precise meaning we need ("bottom line", "launder", "stonewall").
- **Separate good English from technical English.** "Printout" names a real object and stays in its domain; "input" escaped into "I'd like your input on whether God exists". Offer ideas and hear what someone thinks; don't give input and get feedback.
- Nouns turned into verbs overnight ("target goals", "access facts", "the train won't platform", "the door is alarmed", "grow the business") deserve suspicion.
- **Good usage means using good words, if they already exist (they almost always do), to say something clearly and simply to someone else.** Stand watch on the beach and ask of every new piece of flotsam: *Do we need it?*
