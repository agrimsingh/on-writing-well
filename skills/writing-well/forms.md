## Nonfiction as Literature

- Most of what writers write, publishers publish and readers demand is nonfiction. Since the war (and television) made readers fact-minded, **nonfiction has become the new literature**: writers who come bearing information and present it with **vigor, clarity and humanity**.
- Don't accept the snobbery that nonfiction is "only journalism". Journalism is writing that first appears in any periodical, and historically good journalism becomes good literature (Mencken, Lardner, Joseph Mitchell, Edmund Wilson, Lewis Thomas's essays for a medical journal).
- For most people learning to write, nonfiction is the most comfortable path: they can write about what they know, observe or find out. **Motivation is at the heart of writing.**
- **The only important distinction is between good writing and bad writing.**

## Writing About People: The Interview

### Why quotes matter
- **Get people talking.** Ask questions that draw out what is most interesting or vivid in their lives. Nothing animates writing like someone telling what they think or do **in their own words**.
- Their words beat yours, however elegant you are. They carry the speaker's inflection, idiosyncratic syntax, regionalisms, trade lingo and enthusiasms. As soon as the writer steps in, the experience becomes secondhand.
- **Any nonfiction comes alive in proportion to the number of quotes you weave in.**
- **Find the human element in "lifeless" subjects.** Behind every storm sewer is a politician whose future depends on it and a widow outraged that it will wash her block away. Zinsser's institutional histories (the New York Public Library's curators, Sotheby's department heads, the Book-of-the-Month Club judges) became stories about people. Information locked in people's heads is what a good nonfiction writer must unlock.

### Choosing a subject
- Push into the real world. Don't interview your roommate. Pretend you're writing for a real publication.
- Pick someone whose job is important, interesting or unusual enough that readers want to know about it: the pizza-parlor owner, the fisherman, the Little League manager, the nurse, the candlestick maker. **Someone who touches some corner of the reader's life.**

### Conducting the interview
- Interviewing improves with practice. Much of it is mechanical; the rest is instinct about when to relax the subject, push, listen or stop.
- **Be prepared**: bring pencils and paper that work.
- **Keep the notebook out of sight at first.** Chat, gauge the person, earn trust.
- **Do your homework.** Know the official's voting record or the actress's roles. You'll be resented for asking what you could have learned in advance.
- **Make a list of questions** so you don't go dry. Then follow intuition: drag a straying subject back, or follow a better tangent and forget the list.
- **Don't fear imposing.** Most people lead lives "of desperate quietness" and are delighted someone wants to hear about their work. If the first session is stiff, come back another day.
- **Your first answer is seldom sufficient.** Ask "Why not?" and "What else?" until you understand. (See "Enjoyment, fear and confidence" in [attitudes.md](attitudes.md).)
- **The best material often comes after you put the pencil away.** At the leave-taking ask, "Have I seen everything?"

### Notes vs. tape recorder
- Tape is invaluable for oral history and anthropology (Studs Terkel), and Q&A transcripts have pleasing spontaneity. **But strictly it isn't writing.** It's asking questions and then pruning and splicing transcripts, which takes enormous labor. Educated people who seemed to speak in linear sentences turn out not to have finished one; the ear forgives what the eye won't.
- Practical problems: you often won't have it with you, it malfunctions, and above all **a writer should be able to see his materials** rather than shuttle a tape back and forth hunting for a quote. **Be a writer. Write things down.**
- **Exception:** use a recorder where note-taking might violate the cultural integrity of the speaker, to get idiom and tonalities right (Zinsser's book about two Black jazz musicians).
- When the subject talks faster than you can write, say **"Hold it a minute, please"** and catch up. Nobody wants to be misquoted.
- Develop shorthand. **Right after the interview, fill in missing words and complete sentences** while they're within recall. At home, type up your notes so you can review them calmly and discover what the person really said.

### Distilling and fair play
- Much of what was said will be dull, irrelevant or repetitive. Pick the most important or colorful sentences. Using everything because you labored to get it is self-indulgence. **Your job is to distill the essence.**
- **Two standards: brevity and fair play.**
  - **Duty to the speaker:** present their position accurately. Don't quote one side of a carefully weighed view as if it were their verdict. Don't quote out of context, or take a flashy remark without the serious afterthought. You're dealing with their honor and reputation, and your own.
  - **Duty to the reader:** the tightest package. If a remark on page 5 of your notes amplifies a point on page 2, link them. That may violate the chronology of the interview, but it's true to the intent.
- **Play with quotes fairly**: select, reject, thin, transpose, save a good one for the end. **Don't change words or let cutting distort the context.**
- **Verbatim when the speaker chooses words carefully.** Approximation isn't good enough: nobody wants to see themselves using words they'd never use. If speech is ragged (trailing sentences, disordered thoughts, tangles that would embarrass them), **clean up the English and supply the missing links.**
- **Don't be the prisoner of your quotes.** A quote that sounded felicitous may have a hole in its logic. Add a clarifying word or two, find another quote that makes the point, or **call the person back** and have them rephrase it until you understand. **Never let anything out that you don't understand.**

### Organizing the interview piece
- **The lead should say why the person is worth reading about**: their claim on our time and attention.
- **Balance their words and yours.** Three or four consecutive paragraphs of quotation become monotonous. Break quotes up with useful appearances as guide. You're still the writer; don't relinquish control.
- Don't insert dreary filler to break up quotes ("He tapped his pipe on a nearby ashtray…", "She toyed idly with her arugula salad").
- **Start the sentence with the quotation:**
  - BAD: Mr. Smith said that he liked to "go downtown once a week and have lunch with some of my old friends."
  - GOOD: "I usually like to go downtown once a week," Mr. Smith said, "and have lunch with some of my old friends."
  - Opening with "Mr. Smith said" is where many readers stop. Let him say it, warmly and humanly.
- **Break the quote as soon as it's natural**, so readers know who's talking, but not where it damages rhythm or meaning ("I usually like," Mr. Smith said, "to go…" is bad).
- **Attribution verbs:** don't strain for synonyms for "said" (asserted, averred, expostulated). **Never "he smiled" or "he grinned"**; nobody can smile a sentence. Readers' eyes skip "said". For variety, choose verbs that carry real meaning: "pointed out", "explained", "replied", "added". Use "added" only for an actual postscript.

### The ethics of quotation (Mitchell standard)
- Fabricating quotes, or writing scenes from what you "inferred" to be a subject's point of view without interviewing them, assaults the craft.
- **Joseph Mitchell's "Mr. Hunter's Grave"** was spliced from months of conversations into one "afternoon". Every word and turn of phrase was Mr. Hunter's, though. Mitchell used a dramatist's prerogative to **compress time and focus the story** (real time would have the numbing truth of an eight-hour film of a man sleeping), but **never manipulated Mr. Hunter's truth.** No inferring, no fabricating. He played fair.
- **The standard:** some juggling and eliding of quotes is unavoidable (distrust any writer who claims otherwise). **Fabricating quotes or surmising what someone might have said is wrong. Writing is a public trust.** Handle what people tell you as a valuable gift.

## Writing About Places: The Travel Article

- **People and places are the twin pillars of nonfiction.** Every event happens somewhere, and readers want to know what that somewhere was like. The setting might be a sketched paragraph, the mood of a neighborhood, or (in travel writing) the main substance.

### The two traps
1. **Enthusiasm and the agglomeration of detail.** Nobody becomes a bore faster than a traveler home from his travels. We want *some*, not all. What made your trip different? What don't we know already? Not that the Grand Canyon is awesome or Venice has canals. (If somebody fell into the Grand Canyon, *that* would be worth hearing.) Keep a tight rein on your subjective self and an objective eye on the reader. **Mere detail is no free pass; the detail must be significant.**
2. **Style: travelese.** Syrupy words and groaning platitudes: "wondrous", "dappled", "roseate", "fabled", "scudding". Everything is "quaint" (windmills, covered bridges), hill towns are always "nestled", byways are "half forgotten", "old meets new", storefronts smile and ruins beckon. Soft words that mean nothing or mean different things to different people: "attractive", "charming", "romantic" ("one man's romantic sunrise is another man's hangover").

### The two principles
- **Style: choose words with unusual care.** If a phrase comes easily, distrust it; it's probably a travel cliché. Don't strain for the luminous lyrical phrase about the wondrous waterfall either; you'll sound artificial or pompous. **Strive for fresh words and images.** Leave "myriad" to the poets and "ilk" to anyone who will take it away.
- **Substance: be intensely selective.** Eliminate known attributes (shores are rocky, seagulls fly over, the sea has waves, the sand is white). Find details that are **significant**: important to the narrative, unusual, colorful, comic or entertaining. Make sure they do useful work.

### What good place-writing does (models)
- **Joan Didion (San Bernardino Valley):** the drive past the "SLEEP IN A WIGWAM" motel, the drag strip and subdivision flags. Names, statistics and signs all do useful work, conveying the landscape's tackiness and the pathetic impermanence of the lives there.
- **John McPhee (Juneau and Anchorage):** concrete detail as anchor (railings for lawmakers to haul themselves to work in the wind, wind gauges torn apart) and **capturing the idea of a place**: Anchorage is "that part of any city where the city has burst its seams and extruded Colonel Sanders… condensed, instant Albuquerque."
- **Your main task: find the central idea of the place.** Jonathan Raban saw the Mississippi from the air as "a theological problem": square, right-thinking Lutheran farms crossed by a serpentine river, the wild beast in the heart of the heartland.
- **Every place has a look, a population and a set of cultural assumptions.** Find the distinctive traits: Jack Agueros's East Harlem blocks with their invisible boundaries; Prudence Mackintosh's East Texas, more Southern than "Texan"; Tom Wolfe's Muroc desert, "like some fossil landscape".
- **Go often enough to isolate what makes a place distinctive**, usually a mix of the place and its people (a bowling alley's atmosphere plus its regulars; a city's ancient culture plus its present populace). You needn't go to Mombasa. A mall or day-care center will do.
- **Distill the important from the immaterial.** V. S. Pritchett on Istanbul: Turkey's two breeds, those who carry and those who sit. "His very face sits." One quick insight catches a national trait.
- **Let the place draw the best out of you.** In the English tradition, a place generates ideas about how people work, play, worship, live and die (Lawrence, Freya Stark, Thesiger). *Walden* was written a mile from town.
- **What finally brings a place alive is human activity**: people doing the things that give it character (James Baldwin's boyhood church).
- **No place is used up.** "It's not your place until you write about it." For *American Places* (Mount Rushmore, Niagara, the Alamo), Zinsser didn't ask tourists "What do you feel?" (subjective, useless). He asked **custodians** why millions come and what quest they're on, to **enter into the intention of each place**: what it's trying to be, not what you expected. Rangers, curators and old-timers have "the routine eloquence of people who work at a place that fills a need for someone else."
- **Beware of waxing.** At sacred or meaningful places, let others supply the emotion. The USS *Arizona* still leaks oil; the superintendent let children visit because "they'll remember it if they see that the ship is still bleeding." No writerly emoting needed.

## Writing About Yourself: The Memoir

### Permission
- **The subject you know best is yourself**, and it's the one writers most avoid. Students say "We have to write what the teacher wants." Adults say "We have to write what editors want." Lifelong journalists say "I never had the nerve." **You get permission by being born.**
- **Permission isn't permissiveness.** No sloppy, let-it-all-hang-out verbiage. But don't be eager to please. **Write for a teacher or editor and you'll write for nobody; write for yourself and you'll reach the people you want.**
- **Writing is a powerful search mechanism.** Forgotten incidents click into place just when needed. Memory is good for material when other wells go dry.
- **Warning:** excessive writing about yourself can be hazardous to writer and reader. **Ego is healthy; egotism is a drag.** It's not a license for therapeutic prattle. **Make sure every component (people, places, events, anecdotes, ideas, emotions) does useful work and moves the story along.**

### The form
- **Memoir's power is narrowness of focus.** Autobiography spans a whole life; **memoir assumes the life and ignores most of it.** It takes us to one intense corner: childhood, a war, a social upheaval. (Russell Baker's *Growing Up* is a box within a box, a boy's story inside a Depression family's story. Nabokov's *Speak, Memory* is a vanished St. Petersburg boyhood. Pritchett recalls a near-Dickensian apprenticeship without self-pity.)
- **Think narrow.** Memoir isn't the summary of a life; it's **a window into a life**, like a photograph in its selective composition. It may look casual but is a deliberate construction (Thoreau wrote seven drafts of *Walden* in eight years). **Become the editor of your own life**, imposing a narrative shape and organizing idea on an untidy sprawl of half-remembered events. **Memoir is the art of inventing the truth.**

### The secrets
- **Detail.** Any detail works (a sound, a smell, a song title) if it played a **shaping role** in the part of your life you're distilling.
  - *Sound:* Eudora Welty grew up "to the striking of clocks" up and down the stairwell. In a paragraph we learn her home, her father (a lover of instruments that instruct and fascinate) and the origin of her sense of chronology and atmosphere.
  - *Smell:* Alfred Kazin follows smell back to Brooklyn: coffee cake on Friday, his father's overalls smelling of turpentine and shellac, fresh newsprint carrying the world from across the bridge. Memoir is nourished by a **sense of place**: what made a neighborhood and heritage distinctive.
- **Heritage and the immigrant voice.** Memoir is the perfect form for being a newcomer, for the tug of the abandoned past and for cultural identity: Enrique Hank Lopez's "pocho" from Bachimba, Maxine Hong Kingston's kindergarten silence, Lewis P. Johnson's search for his Indian identity. Each brings a voice only that writer could supply.
- **People.** Sounds, smells and sleeping porches go only so far. **Summon back the people who notably crossed your life.** What turn of mind or crazy habits made them memorable? (John Mortimer's blind barrister father in his first-class carriage: "Speak up, Kath!")
- **The most interesting character should be the writer.** What did you learn from the hills and valleys of life? Highly personal forms (journals, diaries, letters) clarify thought and help readers wrestling with similar angels and demons. (We often discover in a letter's third paragraph what we really want to say.) Honesty and vulnerability seize attention.
- **Settle the voice and tense in advance.** Are you the child then or the adult now bringing wisdom to childhood's credulities? There's no single right answer, but readers won't tolerate vacillating between two perspectives and tones.
- **The best gift you have to offer in personal history is the gift of yourself.** Give yourself permission, and have a good time doing it.

## Science and Technology

Also applies to technical documentation, explanations, tutorials, how-tos, architecture docs and READMEs.

### Core principle
- Two symmetric fears: liberal-arts students fear science ("no head for science"), and scientists and engineers fear writing ("no gift for words"). Both are unnecessary. **Writing is thinking on paper. Anyone who thinks clearly can write clearly, about anything.** Science demystified is just another nonfiction subject; writing demystified is just another way to transmit what you know.
- **It's a matter of putting one sentence *after* another. The "after" is crucial.** Nowhere else must sentences form so strict a **linear sequence**. No fanciful leaps or implied truths. **Fact and deduction are the ruling family.**

### The assignment: describe how something works
- A sewing machine, a pump, why an apple falls, how the eye tells the brain what it sees. It forces you to **make sure you know how it works**, then to **take the reader through the same sequence of ideas and deductions** that made it clear to you. It cures disorderly thinking: a fuzzy student who wrote a sequential piece on fire extinguishers (three kinds of fire, three kinds of extinguisher) became a clear writer overnight.
- **"The reader knows nothing."** Never assume readers know what you assume everybody knows, or remember what was once explained. (The airline life jacket: "simply" pull the toggles… The only step you're sure of is inflating it too soon.)
- **Lead readers who know nothing, step by step, to a grasp of subjects they thought they couldn't understand.** This applies to all nonfiction.

### The upside-down pyramid
- **Start at the narrow bottom with the one fact the reader must know before learning anything more.** Each following sentence broadens the last, moving from fact to **significance** and **speculation**: how the discovery changes what was known, what research it opens, where it might be applied. Readers grasp broad implications only if they start with one narrow fact.
- **Model:** Harold Schmeck's page-1 *Times* story begins with a chimpanzee playing tic-tac-toe whose right or wrong moves could be predicted from its brain waves. Each paragraph answers the reader's next question: Why does it matter? Is it new? Where does it lead? Is it isolated? What's the goal? Only then come the details of the method.

### Making it human and visible
- **Find the human element** (or the next-highest rung, the chimp).
- **Use yourself.** Connect the mechanism to the reader's own life. Will Bradbury opens an article on memory with his own earliest memory, sand thrown in his face, then broadens.
- **Weave the science around someone else.** Berton Roueché's medical detective stories: a victim, a mystery, a gumshoe. Start with a man, and not just any man but a **blue** one, not with the history of toxicology.
- **Relate the unfamiliar to what readers can picture.** Reduce the abstract principle to an image. Moshe Safdie explains form in nature through the nautilus shell, the vulture's wing as a space frame, maple leaves vs. cactus, the giraffe's neck. With every hard principle, give a simple illustration.
- **Precise, relatable detail**, as in Diane Ackerman on bat echolocation: 200,000 vibrations a second versus our 20,000, bats that can register a beetle walking on sand or a moth flexing its wings. **Always start with too much material, then give the reader just enough.**
- **Write like a person, not like a scientist.** A scholarly discipline is no excuse for dry pedantry. Loren Eiseley's love affair with nature and Lewis Thomas's love of the cell show that **scientists can write as well as anyone**. Rachel Carson wasn't a "writer"; she was a marine biologist who wrote well because she thought clearly and had a passion for her subject.
- **Read the scientists who write well** in your field (Primo Levi, Peter Medawar, Oliver Sacks, Stephen Jay Gould, Freeman Dyson) and imitate their linear style, avoidance of jargon and constant relating of the arcane to the visible.
- Most physics PhDs who "can't write" won't learn the simple tools of English, precision instruments as refined as any lab's. Robert Keyes on the transistor begins with the ten million transistors in the computer he's writing on, costing less than its keyboard, then poses the question: how much longer can this go on?
- **Never forget where you left readers in the previous paragraph and what they want to know next.** The award-winning *IEEE Spectrum* piece on Iraq's bomb program answers each implied question ("But why is it so messy?" "Has it ever worked?"), explains technical terms quickly ("calutron") or leaves them for a quick lookup ("fissile"), keeps the people (bomb-makers and high-tech cops) and prizes the quotable metaphor ("Suddenly we found a live dinosaur").
- An engineering editor's rule: **"If you can't describe something in words, our own people can't understand each other."** Clarity for specialists across subfields is clarity for everyone.
- **Civic stakes:** on issues where science meets politics, economics and ethics, only clear writing by experts lets citizens make educated choices.

## Business Writing: Writing in Your Job

Applies to memos, letters, reports, proposals, email, announcements, release notes, customer communications, executive messages and internal newsletters.

- **Take workplace writing seriously.** Careers rise or fall on the ability to state facts, summarize a meeting or present an idea coherently.
- **People who work for institutions needn't write like one.** Fear makes their sentences lack humanity, so their institutions do too. **Readers identify with people, not abstractions** ("profitability"), Latinate nouns ("utilization", "implementation") or inert constructions where nobody does anything ("pre-feasibility studies are in the paperwork stage").
- **Orwell's Ecclesiastes:** "the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong…" becomes "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity…". The first invites reading: short words with air around them, the rhythms of speech, vivid images (race, battle, bread, riches) and a person doing something ("I returned and saw"). The second is clotted and tells us a ponderous mind is at work. **We don't even start to read.**

### The Greenwich principals (dejargonizing)
- A principal's letter promising a "special phone communication system to provide additional opportunities for parent input", to "plan and implement an educational plan" with "open dialogue, feedback and sharing of information" (three ways of saying one thing). What the parent wants to hear: *we're making it easier to call teachers; please call often about how your child is doing.* "He doesn't sound like a person… He is squandering a rich resource: himself."
- A "Principal's Greeting" with two paragraphs: the **educator** ("enhanced positive learning environments", "study skills areas", "desired educational objectives") versus the **person** ("Keep informed", "let us know", "I have met many of you", "Please continue to stop in", "I look forward"). Writing about human details (traffic at pickup, cafeteria manners), people wrote with humanity. Explaining their educating, they vanished.
- Ask of every jargon phrase: *what exactly does it mean, and how does it differ from the plain one?* ("study skills areas" vs. "subjects"; "enhanced positive learning environments" vs. "an atmosphere conducive to learning").
- **The fix:** the four articles of faith (**clarity, simplicity, brevity, humanity**), active verbs, no concept nouns, and no professional vocabulary as a crutch. "Evaluative procedures for the objectives were also established based on acceptable criteria" became **"At the end of the year we will evaluate our progress"** or **"We will see how well we have succeeded."**
- Once administrators rise high enough, nobody points out the beauty of a simple declarative sentence again.

### Corporate fog
- "Effective March 30 we will be migrating our end user order entry and supplies referral processing to a new telemarketing center" means *we have a new 800 number* (and "end user" means you). An unclear, impersonal organization loses friends, customers and money ("a shortfall will be experienced in anticipated profitability").
- A customer bulletin: "capacity planning techniques" means *it helps to know when you're giving your computer more than it can handle*. "Adds objectivity to the decision-making process" means *know the facts before you decide*. "Enhanced decision participation" means *the more you know about your system, the better it works*. "Delivered with functionality" means *it works*. Customers won't keep translating; they'll think "If these guys are so smart, why can't they tell me what they do?" Contrast the satisfied customer's line: **"A computer is like a sophisticated pencil. You don't care how it works, but if it breaks you want someone there to fix it."** Comfortable words, a visible image, humanity. Also the subway sign: "These are only new names for very familiar trains."
- **Vanity is the obstacle.** Managers think a simple style reflects a simple mind. **A simple style is the result of hard work and hard thinking; a muddled style reflects a muddled thinker, or one too arrogant, dumb or lazy to organize their thoughts.**
- **What you write is often your only chance to present yourself** to someone whose business, money or goodwill you need. If it's ornate, pompous or fuzzy, that's how you'll be perceived.

### Warming up an institution
- Newsletter prose like "Announced concurrently with the above enhancements were changes to the System Support Program… dynamic reconfiguration and inter-systems communications" informs and cheers nobody.
- **Find the people behind the achievements.** Go to the engineer, designer or technician and get them to say in their own words how the idea came, how they built it and how real people will use it. **"The way to warm up any institution is to locate the missing 'I.'" "I" is the most interesting element in any story.**
- **When experts speak in acronyms, keep going back until they're intelligible.** Your responsibility is to the facts and the reader, not the engineer's vanity among peers. Don't relinquish control.
- **Executives whose messages are ghostwritten** should be interviewed until they talk about their concerns as they would at home. Most executives surrender the qualities that make them unique by letting their words be "pumped up and dried out". Words are among their most powerful tools.
- **Whatever your job or level, be yourself when you write.** You'll stand out as a real person among the robots.

## Sports

- **Sports English is not a separate language.** Write good English. "Southpaw", "circuit clout", "twin killing", "hurler", "pigskin", "hoopsters" and "grapplers" were once fresh, but decades of repetition made them cheaper than the coins they replaced. People read to learn who won, not with enjoyment.
- **Red Smith** avoided exhausted synonyms (he let a batter hit into a double play) and put freshness elsewhere: precise, unusual, good English words where no other sportswriter would put them ("if baseball started with Little Caesar, it wound up with Ethelred the Unready"). A graceful style strong enough to carry strong convictions.
- **Repeat the clearest noun.** Fear of repeating "batter", "runner" or "golfer" breeds the disease where Bob Hornsby becomes "the gangling junior", "the Memphis native", "the Exeter graduate", "the racquet ace" and "the redhead" within a paragraph. Readers don't know them in disguise and don't care. **Never be afraid to repeat the player's name.** A set needn't become a "stanza". The cure is worse than the ailment.
- **Numbers:** mention the significant ones (a 20th win, a 61, a 3:48 mile). Don't run a statistics dump; the UPI story listing SEC records, broken and nearly broken, is unreadable.
- **Humanity makes sportswriting literature** (McPhee, Plimpton, George Will, Updike's "Hub Fans Bid Kid Adieu"). **Updike steered toward the unsolved mystery of the man** (baseball as a lonely game suited to a loner), not the swing everybody knew. **Look for the human bond** between writer and subject. **Hold the hype; give us believable heroes** (Creamer's *Babe* and *Stengel*).
- **Sports is social history**: drug abuse, violence, women's rights, minorities in management, TV money, the financial seduction of student athletes. If you want to write about America, pitch your tent here.
- **Money and ego** have infected athletes and the sportswriters who think *they* are the story. The would-be Maupassants bury the result under exquisitely delayed, "color"-stuffed leads (the grandmother's fortune-cookie dream). The would-be Freuds psychoanalyze athletes ("mortality denial", "predictably futile") or play team doctor ("His condition is day-to-day." Whose isn't?). Sarcasm isn't fact; no team takes the field determined to lose. **Athletes are doing something immensely difficult and have their pride. Your codes of honor include: you are not the story.**
- **Baseball is a game little boys play** (Red Smith). Readers are still those kids in imagination. **Tell us how the players played and how the game came out.**
- **New roles:** convey **what it feels like** to perform a sport (Lesley Hazleton's precise physiology of Formula One g-forces: the face distending into a ghostly smile, pulses of 180–200). Chronicle the **emergence of women athletes and reporters** (Janice Kaplan on marathon times and King vs. Riggs). Ponder the forces altering sport (Bill Bradley's *Life on the Run*).
- **Values to look for: people and places, time and transition.** Hang around the track, stable, stadium and rink. Observe closely. Interview in depth. Listen to old-timers. Ponder the changes. Write well.

## Writing About the Arts: Critics and Columnists

- Journalistic arts writing (the new conductor, the new museum's architect) uses the same methods as any nonfiction. **Appraising a work from the inside requires special skills and knowledge**: being a critic.
- **Temptations:** strutting college words ("intuit", "sensibility", "Kafkaesque") and seeing more symbolism in Ibsen than Ibsen did. Cruel epigrams are easy ("ran the gamut of emotions from A to B"), but true wit is rare, and the only surviving epigrams are cruel ones. **It's easier to bury Caesar than praise him. Saying why something is good, in words that don't sound banal, is one of the hardest chores in the business.**

### Reviewer vs. critic
- **Reviewers** cover an *industry* (TV, movies, mass-market books) for a newspaper or popular magazine. The job is more to **report** than to make aesthetic judgments: deputy for the average person who wants to know what the series is about, whether the movie suits the kids, whether the book delivers. Think about what you'd want to know before spending on the movie, the sitter and dinner.
- **Critics** perform a **serious intellectual act**: evaluating serious works and **placing them in the context** of what came before in the medium or by the artist.

### Rules for both
1. **Love the medium.** If you think movies are dumb, don't review them. Bring knowledge, passion and prejudice. **Go wanting to like it.** The critic who prides himself on hating everything gets tiresome.
2. **Don't give away too much plot.** Give enough for readers to decide if it's their kind of story; often one sentence will do.
3. **Use specific detail, not generalities.** "The play is always fascinating" means nothing; show how. Vague: "mistakes bloodlessness for taste". Precise: "finds portents in lampshades and meanings in table settings". In book reviews, **let the author's words document themselves**: quote the gaudy sentences rather than calling the style gaudy. Describe the set's levels and lighting; put readers in your seat.
4. **Avoid ecstatic adjectives** ("enthralling", "luminous"). Criticism needs a lean, vivid style.

### Criticism proper
- **Steep yourself in the medium's literature and history** (Shakespeare to Sondheim, Bach to Philip Glass) so you can tell the pioneer from the imitator.
- With an urbane readership you can presuppose shared knowledge. **Generate a provocative idea and throw it onto the page.** Readers may disagree (that's their fun), but they enjoy the turn of your mind and the journey to your conclusion. **We like critics as much for personality as opinions.**
- **Make connections across time** (Molly Haskell: Streep's chameleonic disguise vs. the fixed core identity of Bette Davis and the old stars).
- **Disturb when necessary.** Michael Arlen's TV columns compared Vietnam coverage to a child's view through a keyhole. Critics should be among the first to notify us when the truths we hold to be self-evident cease to be true.
- **Hard arts (dance, music):** don't hide behind jargon (rubato, tessitura). Virgil Thomson wrote zestful plain English, was fearless with sacred cows, and diagnosed Toscanini's "wow technique".
- **Humor as lubricant**, but organic, not a few rabbit punches (John Leonard on Michener: "as sincere as shoes… he drives the cattle of his facts until they drop").
- **Warm up the water.** Orient readers to the special world they're entering, even educated readers (Cynthia Ozick's recall of Eliot's colossal campus presence before charting his decline). Criticism that stirs no combative juices is hardly worth writing.

### Columns, essays, editorials
- These forms share one thing: **personal opinion.** Even the editorial "we" was written by an "I". A political columnist must love politics.
- **Express your opinion firmly. Don't cancel it with last-minute evasions.** The most boring sentence in the paper is the editorial's last: "It is too early to tell…", "…remains to be seen." Everything remains to be seen. **Take your stand with conviction.** ("Let's not go peeing down both legs." The most inelegant and probably the best advice Zinsser received.)

## Humor

- **Humor is the nonfiction writer's secret weapon**, often the best and sometimes the only tool for making an important point. If you're trying to write humor, almost everything you do is serious.
- Serious humorists (Ade, Mencken, Lardner, Perelman, Buchwald, Feiffer, Woody Allen, Keillor) force the country to see itself clearly. **One strong editorial cartoon is worth a hundred solemn editorials.** *Catch-22* and *Dr. Strangelove* heightened the truth about war just enough to catch its lunacy. **The joke is no joke.**
- **The essence: heighten some crazy truth to a level where it's seen as crazy.**
- **Sermons are the death of humor.** Find a comic device (satire, parody, irony, lampoon, nonsense) to disguise the serious point. Often it takes a long time. Zinsser spent a year on women wearing hair curlers everywhere before finding four hair magazines and inventing the parody advice column "Haircurl". Afterward readers can never look at curlers the same way: **jolted into a fresh look at something bizarre that was taken for granted.**
- **Control is vital.** Parody works by sticking close to the form being parodied (style and mentality). Don't use comic names like "Throttlebottom". Don't repeat the same kind of joke; make it once. Trust sophisticated readers and don't worry about the rest.
- **Purpose:** "Something crazy is going on here, some erosion in the quality of life or threat to life itself, and yet everyone assumes it's normal." The outlandish becomes routine overnight; the humorist insists it's still outlandish. Humorists have helped bring down LBJ, McCarthy, Agnew and Boss Tweed.
- **"I'm here and I'm involved"** (Mort Sahl). Make that your creed. Humorists must go against the grain and say what people and presidents don't want to hear.
- **Humor about the ordinary** (heart, home, family, job) is endless. Chic Young built *Blondie* on four things everybody does: **sleeping, eating, raising a family and making money.** Most humor rests on fundamental truths. It isn't a separate organism. **It's a special angle of vision granted to writers who already write good English**, about life that is essentially serious, at the points where serious hopes are mocked: "the strange incongruity between our aspiration and our achievement" (Leacock). Humor is "a by-product that occurs in the serious work of some" (E. B. White).

### Principles for the humorist
1. **Master good "straight" English first.** Humorists from Twain to Russell Baker are superb writers.
2. **Find what's funny in what you know to be true** rather than hunting the outlandish.
3. **Don't strain for laughs.** Humor is built on surprise, and you can surprise only so often.
4. **Humor is subjective.** One editor's dud is another's jewel. Dissect it and, like a frog, it dies (White).

### Lessons from the tradition (and the Yale humor course)
- Forms are flexible: fable in slang with capitalized slang (George Ade), dramatic dialogue and mock stage directions (Lardner), doggerel by a cockroach who can't work the shift key (Don Marquis's *Archy*), free association (Benchley, Perelman), parody of genres and disciplines (Woody Allen, Keillor's G-men hunting the last smokers, Huns invading Chicago during the S&L bailout), deadpan reporting stuffed with outlandish true facts and quotes (Mark Singer), pure nonsense (Ian Frazier, Updike's "Glad Rags", which works because of meticulous research).
- **Humor deflects anger** into a channel where we laugh at frailty instead of railing against it (Marquis).
- **"All humor must be about something; it must touch concretely on life"** (Perelman). Some pomposity lies in ruins at the end.
- **The humorist as victim or dunce** (Leacock's "My Financial Career") is therapy for readers, who feel superior or identify with a fellow victim. A humorist of ordinary life never runs out of material (Erma Bombeck).
- **Enjoyment is what humorists must convey**: cranked-up audacity, the feeling the writer is having a terrific time.
- **Sustaining comic invention in other voices is exhausting.** Students moved from imitating Woody Allen to writing in their own voice about their own lives (the Chic Young principle: stick to what you know).
- Problems came mainly from **exaggeration**: cut the extra sentence that explains an already implicit funny point, and judge how much enlargement is allowed. **Start from the truth rather than invention** (Thurber's bed really did fall, more or less). The class began striving for humor and hoping to wing a few truths, and ended striving for truth and hoping to add humor. **The two are intertwined.**
