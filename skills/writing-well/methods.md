## Unity

- **You learn to write by writing.** Produce a set number of words on a regular schedule. A reporter writing two or three pieces a day improves within six months: not necessarily writing *well*, but gaining confidence and finding the common problems.
- **All writing is problem-solving**: where to get facts, how to organize, approach, attitude, tone, style. Experience means you've "been there before", like a surgeon on his 500th appendix.
- **Unity is the anchor of good writing.** It keeps readers from straggling, satisfies their subconscious need for order and reassures them that all is well at the helm. Choose among the variables and stick to your choice.

### The unities
| Unity | Choice | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Pronoun | First person (participant), third person (observer), or rarely second person | Shifting viewpoint; the Hemingway-imitating "you knew this had to be…" |
| Tense | The tense you *principally* address the reader in. Past is usual; present can be agreeable. | Switching back and forth. (Glances backward or forward are what tenses exist for.) |
| Mood | Casual (*New Yorker* style) or formal for serious events and important facts. Any tone is acceptable. | Mixing two or three tones |

**The fatal mixture (Hong Kong example).** A travel writer starts as a real person ("My wife, Ann, and I had always wanted to visit Hong Kong…"), turns into a brochure ("Hong Kong affords many fascinating experiences to the curious sightseer… quaint funicular"), comes back to Ann and the restaurants, then becomes a guidebook ("To enter Hong Kong it is necessary to have a valid passport…"). The writer is gone, so is Ann, and soon so are we. The sampans and hepatitis shots weren't wrong to include. The writer just never decided what kind of article to write. **The material controlled him instead of the other way round.** Had he stayed conversational, he could have woven in the ferry and the weather naturally.

### Questions to ask before you start
1. In what capacity am I addressing the reader? (Reporter? Information provider? Average man or woman?)
2. What pronoun and tense?
3. What style? (Impersonal reportorial? Personal but formal? Personal and casual?)
4. What attitude toward the material? (Involved? Detached? Judgmental? Ironic? Amused?)
5. **How much do I want to cover?**
6. **What one point do I want to make?**

### Think small
- Most nonfiction writers have a *definitiveness complex*: they want to write the last word. **There is no last word.** What's definitive today is undefinitive by tonight. Writers chasing every fact chase the rainbow and never sit down to write.
- Nobody writes a book "about" something. Tolstoy didn't write about war and peace, or Melville about whaling. They made reductive decisions about time, place and individuals: **one man pursuing one whale.** Reduce every project before you start.
- **Decide what corner of the subject to bite off, cover it well, and stop.** You'll often find you've said almost everything you wanted about the whole subject.
- **Energy and morale:** an unwieldy task drains enthusiasm, and enthusiasm keeps both you and the reader going. When your zest ebbs, the reader is the first to know.

### The one point
- **Every successful piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with one provocative thought they didn't have before.** Not two or five. Just one.
- It tells you the route and destination, and it shapes tone: some points are best made earnestly, some by dry understatement, some by humor.
- Once the unities are decided, there's no material you can't work into your frame.

### When the plan turns out wrong
- Material often leads somewhere unexpected where a different tone feels more comfortable. That's normal: writing generates thoughts and memories you didn't anticipate.
- **Don't fight the current if it feels right.** Trust your material when it takes you somewhere you didn't intend but the vibrations are good. **Never become the prisoner of a preconceived plan. Writing is no respecter of blueprints.**
- Afterward, part two will be out of joint with part one, but you'll know which part is truest to your instincts. Go back and rewrite the beginning so the mood and style hold from start to finish. Scissors and paste are honorable tools. All the unities must fit into the finished edifice, however backwardly assembled.

## The Lead

- **The most important sentence in any article is the first one.** If it doesn't induce the reader to go on to the second, the article is dead. The same goes for the second sentence, and the third. That progression of sentences, each tugging the reader forward until hooked, is the lead.
- **Length:** no pat answer. Some hook in a few well-baited sentences; others amble for pages with a slow, steady pull. **The only valid test: does it work?** If it does the job, be thankful and proceed.
- Literary-review readers tolerate discursive openings. Don't count on it: readers want to know, very soon, **what's in it for them.**
- Remember where periodicals actually get read (barbershops, salons, airplanes, doctors' offices). More reading happens under the dryer than under the reading lamp, so don't fool around.

### What the lead must do
1. **Capture the reader immediately.** Cajole with freshness, novelty, paradox, humor, surprise, an unusual idea, an interesting fact or a question. Anything that nudges curiosity and tugs at the sleeve.
2. **Do real work.** Give hard details that tell readers why the piece was written and why they should read it. Don't dwell on the reason; keep them inquisitive.
3. **Keep building.** Every paragraph amplifies the one before. Put more thought into solid detail, less into entertaining.
4. **Take special care with each paragraph's last sentence**, the springboard to the next. Give it an extra twist of humor or surprise, like a stand-up comic's periodic "snapper". Make the reader smile and you've got them for at least one more paragraph.

### Model leads
- **"Block That Chickenfurter":** "I've often wondered what goes into a hot dog. Now I know and I wish I didn't." Two short sentences. Then one sentence explaining the news peg (the USDA ingredients ruling), a snapper ("can a chickenfurter find happiness in the land of the frank?"), another fact and another smile (the woman who wrote "I don't eat feather meat of no kind"). **When you're lucky enough to get a quotation that funny, find a way to use it.** The casual style holds, but the content turns out more serious than the whimsical lead promised: Americans don't know or care what goes into their food.
- **"Thank God for Nuts":** a slower lead that pulls with curiosity rather than humor. Burleigh Grimes's slippery elm bark at Cooperstown ("one of the least interesting facts available in America today") pivots to "But baseball fans can't be judged by any reasonable standard." Then personal connection and the larger theme: the museum as a pilgrimage to our past.
  - **Salvation often lies in an odd fact rather than in style.** Zinsser found the bark only on a second visit the next morning.
  - **Always collect more material than you'll use.** An article is strong in proportion to the surplus of detail it can choose from, but at some point you must stop researching and start writing.
  - **Look for material everywhere:** signs, billboards, roadside junk, package labels, toy instructions, medicine claims, graffiti, utility-bill inserts, menus, catalogues, junk mail, the Sunday real-estate section. These quirky messages often make a lead that differs from everyone else's.
- **Joan Didion, "7000 Romaine, Los Angeles 38":** a steady accumulation of facts with pathos and faded glamour (Jean Harlow's manicurist, the WELCOME mat at a locked door) that pulls us toward the riddle of Howard Hughes.
- **Edmund Wilson, Dead Sea Scrolls:** *just tell a story.* Narrative is the oldest and most compelling way to hold attention; everybody wants to be told a story. Look for ways to convey information as narrative. Wilson starts right in ("At some point rather early in the spring of 1947, a Bedouin boy called Muhammed the Wolf was minding some goats…") with no "breakfast-to-bed" stage-setting that opens a fishing trip with an alarm clock.
- **Whole story in one sentence**, from famous nonfiction openings:
  - "In the beginning God created heaven and earth." (the Bible)
  - Churchill: "In the summer of the Roman year 699… Gaius Julius Caesar, turned his gaze upon Britain."
  - *Joy of Cooking*: "Put this puzzle together and you will find milk, cheese and eggs…"
  - Margaret Mead: "To the Manus native the world is a great platter…"
  - Betty Friedan: "The problem lay buried, unspoken, for many years in the minds of American women."
  - Tom Wolfe: "Within five minutes, or ten minutes, no more than that, three of the others had called her…"
  - Dr. Spock: "You know more than you think you do."

### Leads to retire
- **The future archaeologist:** "When some future archaeologist stumbles on the remains of our civilization…"
- **The visitor from Mars:** "If a creature from Mars landed on our planet…"
- **The cute event "one day not long ago"**, the button-nosed boy and his dog in a field outside Paramus.
- **The have-in-common lead:** "What did Stalin, MacArthur, Wittgenstein… have in common? They all loved Westerns."

Give the lead a freshness of perception or detail. There are no firm rules: within the broad rule of not letting the reader get away, approach the subject in whatever way most naturally suits the material and who you are.

## The Ending

- **Knowing when to end is far more important than most writers realize.** Give the last sentence as much thought as the first. Well, almost as much.
- Readers *will* leave near the end if the end in sight turns out to be a mirage, like a minister's sermon building to a series of perfect conclusions that never conclude.
- **Escape the outline's grip.** School taught beginning, middle and end, with a Roman numeral III that summarized the journey. That's fine for students learning logical design, but good nonfiction must wriggle out of III's dread grip.
- **Warning signals:** "In sum, it can be noted that…", "What insights, then, have we been able to glean from…?" These mean you're about to repeat in compressed form what you already said in detail. Readers hear the laborious **sound of cranking**, notice your boredom, resent being treated as too dumb to get the point, and quit.
- **The negative reason:** a misplaced ending wrecks an otherwise tight piece. **The positive reason:** a good last sentence or paragraph is a joy in itself. It gives the reader a lift and lingers.
- **The perfect ending takes readers slightly by surprise yet seems exactly right.** They didn't expect it so soon or so abruptly, or to say what it said, but they know it when they see it. It's like the curtain line of a comedy: the lights go out mid-scene, and we're delighted by the playwright's control.
- **The rule: when you're ready to stop, stop.** If you've presented the facts and made your point, look for the nearest exit.

### Ways to end
- **A few sentences that encapsulate the idea and end with a jolt of fitness or unexpectedness.** Mencken on Coolidge: "There were no thrills while he reigned, but neither were there any headaches. He had no ideas, and he was not a nuisance." Five short sentences send the reader off with an arresting thought.
- **Full circle:** strike at the end an echo of a note sounded at the beginning. It satisfies symmetry and completes the journey with resonance.
- **A quotation (usually works best).** Search your notes for a remark with finality, humor or an unexpected closing detail. Sometimes you know during the interview: "That's my ending!" Woody Allen: "I'm obsessed by the fact that my mother genuinely resembles Groucho Marx." It comes from so far out in left field nobody could see it coming.
- **Surprise is one of the most refreshing elements in nonfiction.** If something surprises you, it will surprise and delight your readers, especially as you send them on their way.

## Bits & Pieces

### Verbs
- **Use active verbs unless there's no comfortable way around a passive.** Active vs. passive style is the difference between life and death for a writer.
- "Joe saw him" is strong. "He was seen by Joe" is weak: longer, insipid, and ambiguous (once? daily?). A passive style saps the reader's energy; nobody knows what is being perpetrated by whom and on whom.
- Passive-voice writers favor long Latinate words over short Anglo-Saxon ones, which makes sentences more glutinous. **Short is generally better than long.** Of the 701 words in Lincoln's Second Inaugural, 505 have one syllable and 122 have two.
- **Verbs are the most important tools.** They push the sentence forward. Active verbs push hard; passives tug fitfully. Active verbs make activity visible because they need a pronoun, noun or person to set them going.
- Many verbs carry their meaning in imagery or sound: glitter, dazzle, twirl, beguile, scatter, swagger, poke, pamper, vex. **Don't choose one that's dull or merely serviceable.**
- **Avoid verbs that need an appended preposition.** Don't "set up" a business you can start or launch.
- **Be precise.** Don't say the president "stepped down". Did he resign, retire or get fired?
- For active verbs at their best, read the King James Bible and Shakespeare, as well as Hemingway, Thurber and Thoreau.

### Adverbs
- **Most adverbs are unnecessary.** Don't pick a verb with a specific meaning and add an adverb that repeats it: "blared loudly", "clenched his teeth tightly".
- The same goes for other parts of speech: "effortlessly easy", "slightly spartan" (a monk's cell with wall-to-wall carpeting?), "totally flabbergasted" (nobody is partly flabbergasted). "Grinned widely" is news we can be spared.
- **Retire "decidedly" and its slippery cousins:** "eminently fair", "arguably the best", "virtually". If it can be proved, omit "arguably". If it's open to argument, say so. (Admittedly it's virtually a toss-up.)

### Adjectives
- **Most adjectives are also unnecessary.** The concept is usually already in the noun: precipitous cliffs, lacy spiderwebs, yellow daffodils, brownish dirt.
- **Make adjectives do work the noun can't:** "garish" daffodils (a value judgment), red dirt where dirt really is red, "the gray sky and the black clouds" when the darkness is the reason he sails back.
- **Adjective-by-habit** (stately elms, frisky kittens, hard-bitten detectives, sleepy lagoons) is self-indulgence for the writer and a burden for the reader. Not every oak has to be gnarled.
- Used sparsely, "drab" and "beautiful" have their proper power.

### Little qualifiers
- **Prune** "a bit", "a little", "sort of", "kind of", "rather", "quite", "very", "too", "pretty much", "in a sense" and dozens more. They dilute style and persuasiveness.
- Don't be a bit confused, sort of tired, a little depressed and somewhat annoyed. **Be confused. Be tired. Be depressed. Be annoyed.** Good writing is lean and confident.
- Not "weren't too happy because the hotel was pretty expensive", but "weren't happy because the hotel was expensive". "Spectacular" and "awesome" don't submit to measurement. "Very" can add emphasis but is usually clutter: either he is methodical or he isn't.
- **It's a matter of authority.** Every qualifier whittles away some of the reader's trust. **Don't be kind of bold. Be bold.**

### Punctuation
- **Period:** most writers don't reach it soon enough. Mired in a long sentence? You're probably asking it to do too much, maybe hold two dissimilar thoughts. Break it into two or three. Among good writers the short sentence predominates. If you write long sentences, be a genius, or at least keep the syntax and punctuation under control from start to finish.
- **Exclamation point:** avoid unless you need a specific effect. It has a gushy, debutante aura ("I could have danced all night!"). Build word order to put the emphasis where you want it. **Never use it to flag a joke or irony**; it robs readers of finding it funny themselves. Humor works best through understatement.
- **Semicolon:** a 19th-century mustiness of balanced "on the one hand… on the other" sentences. Use it sparingly, usually to add a related thought. It slows the pace to Victorian speed. Rely instead on the period and the dash.
- **Dash:** fully respectable, and it gets you out of many tight corners. (1) A single dash amplifies or justifies the first half: "We decided to keep going—it was only 100 miles more…". (2) A pair sets off a parenthetical thought: "She told me to get in the car—she had been after me all summer to have a haircut—and we drove silently into town."
- **Colon:** increasingly antique, but unbeatable for bringing the sentence to a brief halt before an itemized list ("…the following ports: Oran, Algiers, Naples…").

### Mood changers
- **Alert the reader as soon as possible to any change in mood**: "but", "yet", "however", "nevertheless", "still", "instead", "thus", "therefore", "meanwhile", "now", "later", "today", "subsequently". Readers process a shift far more easily when it's announced up front rather than discovered at the end.
- **"But" is the strongest opener.** If you were taught never to start a sentence with "but", unlearn it. It announces total contrast and primes the reader.
- **"However"** is weaker and needs care. Don't start with it (it hangs there like a wet dishrag) or end with it (by then it has lost its howeverness). Put it as early as you reasonably can.
- **"Yet"** works like "but" with the sense of "nevertheless". One short word replaces a long, dismal clause: "Nevertheless he decided to go" vs. "Despite the fact that all these dangers had been pointed out to him, he decided to go." "Instead I took the train." "Still I had to admire him." "Thus I learned how to smoke."
- **"Meanwhile", "now", "today", "later"** prevent confusion when the time frame changes: "Now I know better." "Later I found out why." **Always ask where you left the reader in the previous sentence.**

### Contractions
- Contractions ("I'll", "won't", "can't") make the style warmer and truer when they fit. "I will be glad to see them if they do not get mad" is stilted; read it aloud. Trust your ear.
- Avoid "I'd", "he'd" and "we'd": had or would? Readers can get well into the sentence before learning which, and often guess wrong.
- Don't invent contractions ("could've"). They cheapen the style. Stick with the ones in the dictionary.

### That and which
- **Always use "that" unless it makes the meaning ambiguous.** Carefully edited magazines use "that" far more. "Which" is not more correct or literary.
- **If the sentence needs a comma for its meaning, it probably needs "which".** "Take the shoes that are in the closet" (not the ones under the bed) vs. "Take the shoes, which are in the closet" (one pair, and here's where it is).
- Most "which" clauses describe, identify, locate, explain or qualify what came before the comma: "The house, which has a red roof"; "The Rhine, which is in Germany"; "The moon, which I saw from the porch".

### Concept nouns
- **Concept nouns replace verbs that tell what somebody did.** Dead sentences: "The common reaction is incredulous laughter." "Bemused cynicism isn't the only response to the old system." "The current campus hostility is a symptom of the change."
- They're eerie because they have **no people and no working verbs**, only "is". All the meaning sits in abstract nouns.
- **Turn them around and get people doing things:** "Most people just laugh with disbelief." "Some people respond to the old system by turning cynical; others say…" "It's easy to notice the change: you can see how angry all the students are."
- Don't get caught holding a bag of abstract nouns. You'll sink to the bottom of the lake.

### Creeping nounism
- Two or three nouns strung together where one noun, or better one verb, will do. Nobody goes broke; we have "money problem areas". It doesn't rain; we have "precipitation activity" or a "thunderstorm probability situation". **Please, let it rain.**
- Chains of four or five: "communication facilitation skills development intervention". No person and no verb in sight. (Probably a program to help students write better.)

### Overstatement
- "The living room looked as if an atomic bomb had gone off there." "I felt as if ten 747 jets were flying through my brain." Verbal high jinks bring on overpowering drowsiness, like being trapped with someone who can't stop reciting limericks.
- **Don't overstate.** Life has plenty of truly funny situations. **Let the humor sneak up.**

### Credibility
- As fragile for a writer as for a President. **Don't inflate an incident.** If readers catch one bogus statement passed off as true, everything you write afterward is suspect.

### Dictation (and by extension, unedited drafting)
- Dictated, never-reviewed writing tends to be pompous, sloppy and redundant. You save a few hours and blow your whole personality. At minimum, edit what you dictated (or generated) so it truly reflects who you are, especially for documents customers will judge you and your company by.

### Writing is not a contest
- Every writer starts from a different point and heads somewhere different. The hares who write for the college paper are often overtaken by tortoises steadily mastering the craft. **Forget the competition and go at your own pace. Your only contest is with yourself.**

### The subconscious mind
- It does more writing than you think. A thicket that defeated you all day often clears the next morning; your writer's mind kept working while you slept. Stay alert to the currents around you. What you see and hear percolates and resurfaces just when you need it.

### The quickest fix
- A troublesome phrase is often cured by **removing it.** It's usually the last solution writers think of. First they move it, rephrase it and add words to oil what's stuck, which makes it worse. At an impasse, ask: **"Do I need it at all?"** Probably not; it was doing an unnecessary job all along. Remove it and the sentence springs to life. It's the quickest cure and very often the best.

### Paragraphs
- **Keep paragraphs short.** Writing is visual: it catches the eye before the brain. Short paragraphs put air around the text; a long chunk of type discourages readers from starting.
- Newspaper paragraphs: two or three sentences (narrow columns).
- **But don't go berserk.** A string of tiny paragraphs, the "verbless wonders" of breezy journalism, is as annoying as one too long. It chops a natural train of thought and is condescending ("Yoo-hoo! Look how simple I'm making this for you!"). Rewritten as one logical unit of three sentences, the AP's White House lawyer story gained dignity and good English.
- **Paragraphing is a road map** showing how you organized your ideas. Good nonfiction writers think in paragraph units, not sentence units. Each paragraph has its own integrity of content and structure.

### Nonsexist language
- Avoid words that patronize ("gal"), imply second-class status ("poetess") or role ("housewife"), suggest empty-headedness ("the girls"), demean competence ("lady lawyer") or are prurient ("divorcée", "coed", "blonde"). Men get mugged; a mugged woman becomes a "shapely stewardess" or "pert brunette".
- **Subtler and more damaging:** usages treating women as possessions of the family male. "Early settlers pushed west with their wives and children" becomes *pioneer families*, *pioneer couples who went west with their sons and daughters*, or *men and women who settled the West*. Don't imply only men are settlers, farmers, cops or firefighters.
- **"-man" words:** find another term ("chair", "company representative"), turn the noun into a verb ("Speaking for the company, Ms. Jones said…"), or use a generic substitute ("performers" for actors and actresses). Makeshift coinages like "chairperson" sometimes hurt the cause more than help.
- **The pronoun:**
  - Plurals ("All employees should decide what they think is best…") work in small doses. Converting every "he" to "they" turns the style to mush, and plurals are less specific and harder to visualize.
  - "He or she" sparingly, where it feels natural.
  - **Never "he/she"**; the slant has no place in good English.
  - **Best: eliminate "he" by changing other parts of the sentence.** Use "we" ("First we notice what's happening to our kids and we blame it on the neighborhood"). Swap "our" or "the" for "his". Use general nouns ("Doctors often neglect their families"). Address the reader as **"you"**, a godsend for instructional and self-help writing (the voice of Dr. Spock or Julia Child). Always look for ways to make yourself available to the people you're trying to reach.
  - Zinsser found three or four hundred places in his own book to remove the male pronoun with no harm done.

### Rewriting
- **Rewriting is the essence of writing well: where the game is won or lost.** We have emotional equity in the first draft and can't believe it wasn't born perfect. The odds it wasn't are close to 100 percent.
- The newly hatched sentence almost always has something wrong: not clear, not logical, verbose, klunky, pretentious, boring, full of clutter or clichés, lacking rhythm, readable several ways, not leading out of the previous sentence. **Clear writing is the result of a lot of tinkering.**
- Careful writers can't stop fiddling. **Writing is like a good watch: it should run smoothly and have no extra parts.** Think of rewriting as a gift, not punishment. **Writing is an evolving process, not a product.**
- **What rewriting is:** not writing a different second and third version, but **reshaping, tightening and refining the raw material** of the first try. Much of it ensures a narrative flow the reader can follow with no trouble. Keep putting yourself in the reader's place:
  - Is there something they should have been told early in the sentence that you put near the end?
  - Do they know, when sentence B starts, that you've shifted subject, tense, tone or emphasis from sentence A?

#### The annotated paragraph (editor's thoughts in brackets)
Draft, with editorial notes:

> There used to be a time when neighbors took care of one another, he remembered. *[Put "he remembered" first to establish reflective tone.]* It no longer seemed to happen that way, however. *[The contrast supplied by "however" must come first. Start with "But." Also establish America locale.]* He wondered if it was because everyone in the modern world was so busy. *[All these sentences are the same length and have the same soporific rhythm; turn this one into a question?]* It occurred to him that people today have so many things to do that they don't have time for old-fashioned friendship. *[Sentence essentially repeats previous sentence; kill it or warm it up with specific detail.]* Things didn't work that way in America in previous eras. *[Reader is still in the present; reverse the sentence to tell him he's now in the past.]* And he knew that the situation was very different in other countries, as he recalled from the years when he lived in villages in Spain and Italy. *[Reader is still in America. Use a negative transition word to get him to Europe. Too flabby; break it into two sentences?]* It almost seemed to him that as people got richer and built their houses farther apart they isolated themselves from the essentials of life. *[Irony deferred too long. Plant irony early. Sharpen the paradox about richness.]* And there was another thought that troubled him. *[This is the real point of the paragraph; signal that it's important. Avoid weak "there was" construction.]* His friends had deserted him when he needed them most during his recent illness. *[Reshape to end with "most"; the last word is the one that stays in the reader's ear and gives the sentence its punch. Hold sickness for next sentence.]* It was almost as if they found him guilty of doing something shameful. *[Introduce sickness here as the reason for the shame. Omit "guilty"; it's implicit.]* He recalled reading somewhere about societies in primitive parts of the world in which sick people were shunned, though he had never heard of any such ritual in America. *[Starts slowly and stays sluggish. Break into shorter units. Snap off the ironic point.]*

Revision:

> He remembered that neighbors used to take care of one another. But that no longer seemed to happen in America. Was it because everyone was so busy? Were people really so preoccupied with their television sets and their cars and their fitness programs that they had no time for friendship? In previous eras that was never true. Nor was it how families lived in other parts of the world. Even in the poorest villages of Spain and Italy, he recalled, people would drop in with a loaf of bread. An ironic idea struck him: as people got richer they cut themselves off from the richness of life. But what really troubled him was an even more shocking fact. The time when his friends deserted him was the time when he needed them most. By getting sick he almost seemed to have done something shameful. He knew that other societies had a custom of "shunning" people who were very ill. But that ritual only existed in primitive cultures. Or did it?

**The revision techniques, distilled:**
1. Put the framing or reflective phrase first to set tone.
2. Signal contrast at the start ("But").
3. Establish the locale early so later shifts make sense.
4. Break monotonous rhythm with a question or a short sentence.
5. Kill a sentence that repeats the previous one, or warm it with specific detail ("television sets and their cars and their fitness programs"; "drop in with a loaf of bread").
6. Flag time shifts at the start ("In previous eras…").
7. Use a negative transition to move between places ("Nor was it…").
8. Plant irony early and sharpen paradox ("as people got richer they cut themselves off from the richness of life").
9. Signal the real point ("But what really troubled him was…") and avoid "there was".
10. **End the sentence on its strongest word** ("most").
11. One thought per sentence; give the separate thought its own sentence.
12. Cut implicit words ("guilty").
13. Break sluggish sentences into short units and snap off the ironic point ("Or did it?").

These are mostly carpentry: sequence, flow, point. Cadence, detail and freshness can still improve.

#### Total construction
- Beyond sentence repairs, **read the whole piece aloud, always remembering where you left the reader in the previous sentence.**
- **Reference trap:** "The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions." Fine alone, wrong in sequence: the name lingering in the reader's ear is Othello, so the reader assumes Othello is small and malevolent.
- Reading aloud reveals the places where you lost or confused the reader, omitted the one fact they needed, or said the same thing twice. Make an arrangement that holds together from start to finish and **moves with economy and warmth.**
- **Learn to enjoy tidying.** "I don't like to write (I like to have written). But I love to rewrite." Love cutting (watching a needless word vanish), replacing a humdrum word with a precise or colorful one, strengthening transitions, and rephrasing a drab sentence for rhythm and a more graceful musical line. **It's the rewriting, not the writing, that wins the game.**

### Word processing
- An editor puts words in front of you for instant reconsideration. Play with sentences until they're right. It saves time, output, energy and enjoyment, and gives control, especially for complex organization.

### Trust your material
- **There's nothing more interesting than the truth.** What people do and say keeps surprising with its wonderfulness, quirkiness, drama, humor or pain. Who could invent what really happens?
- **The "color" fallacy.** Reporters wrote feature-style snippet leads ("Whoosh! / It was incredible. / Ed Barnes wondered if he was seeing things…") and readers reached "Continued on page 9" with no idea what the story was about, while page 9 held an interesting story full of specifics. "In the lead I was writing color." **Fact and color aren't separate ingredients; color is organic to the fact. Present the colorful fact.**
- **Don't romanticize or nudge with significance.** Zinsser refused to let his baseball writing "go into slow motion" or claim baseball as a metaphor for life. His premise: baseball is a job, honorable work, and he wanted to know how it gets taught and learned. So he asked the teachers how they teach.
- **Let self-sufficient facts stand.** The scout's 4.3 seconds (a double play takes 4.3 seconds, so a runner who reaches first faster is interesting) needs no sentence pointing out how little time that is. Readers can do their own marveling and enjoy being allowed to think. **The reader plays a major role in the act of writing and must be given room to play it.**
- **Don't over-explain**, telling readers what they know or can surmise. Avoid "surprisingly", "predictably" and "of course", which put a value on a fact before the reader meets it.

### Go with your interests
- **There's no subject you don't have permission to write about.** No area of life is stupid to someone who takes it seriously: skateboarding, cheerleading, rock music, cars, fishing, poker, billiards, rodeos, sea turtles. Write about hobbies, work and old enthusiasms. **No subject is too specialized or quirky if you make an honest connection with it.** Follow your affections and you'll write well and engage readers.
