# Worked Examples

Before-and-after revisions that show the techniques in action. Part A uses Zinsser's own examples from *On Writing Well*. Part B applies the same methods to everyday modern writing: docs, READMEs, PR descriptions, incident emails, status updates, announcements and feedback.

Each example names the problems in brackets, the way Zinsser bracketed his students' clutter: *"I think this can go without losing meaning. You decide."*

# Part A: From the book

## A1. The blackout order (simplicity)

**Before** (a U.S. government memo, 1942):
> Such preparations shall be made as will completely obscure all Federal buildings and non-Federal buildings occupied by the Federal government during an air raid for any period of time from visibility by reason of internal or external illumination.

**After** (Franklin D. Roosevelt's translation):
> Tell them that in buildings where they have to keep the work going to put something across the windows.

**What changed:** a passive, abstract institution ("such preparations shall be made") became a person telling people what to do. The Latinate nouns (*preparations, visibility, illumination*) became a concrete act (*put something across the windows*). *Simplify, simplify.*

## A2. Ecclesiastes vs. bureaucratic fuzz

**The original** (King James Bible):
> I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favor to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all.

**Orwell's "translation" into modern prose:**
> Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the unpredictable must invariably be taken into account.

**Why the first works:**
- It *looks* inviting: short words with air around them.
- It has the rhythms of speech and concrete images: race, battle, bread, riches.
- A person is doing something: "I returned and saw."

The second is clotted with Latin abstractions. We don't even start to read it. It tells us a ponderous mind is at work.

## A3. The school principal (jargon vs. a person)

**Before** (letter to parents):
> We have implemented a special phone communication system to provide additional opportunities for parent input. We will plan and implement an educational plan that ensures open dialogue, feedback and sharing of information.

[*"Implement" twice. "Input", "dialogue", "feedback" and "sharing of information" are four ways of saying one thing. No person in sight.*]

**After:**
> We've made it easier to reach your child's teachers by phone. Please call often. We want to hear how your child is doing, and we'll tell you what we see.

**What changed:** the writer found the one thing the parent needs to know and said it as a person would. "He is squandering a rich resource: himself."

**Before:**
> Evaluative procedures for the objectives were also established based on acceptable criteria.

**After:**
> At the end of the year we will evaluate our progress.

Or simply: *We will see how well we have succeeded.*

## A4. The corporate customer bulletin (fog)

| Bulletin says | Customer hears |
|---|---|
| "Effective March 30 we will be migrating our end user order entry and supplies referral processing to a new telemarketing center." | We have a new 800 number. |
| "Capacity planning techniques" | It helps to know when you're giving your computer more than it can handle. |
| "Adds objectivity to the decision-making process" | Know the facts before you decide. |
| "Enhanced decision participation" | The more you know about your system, the better it works. |
| "Delivered with functionality" | It works. |
| "A shortfall will be experienced in anticipated profitability." | We'll make less money than we expected. |

**The human version** (a satisfied customer, quoted): "A computer is like a sophisticated pencil. You don't care how it works, but if it breaks you want someone there to fix it." Comfortable words, a picture and a person.

## A5. Concept nouns turned into people

| Dead (concept nouns, only "is") | Alive (people doing things) |
|---|---|
| The common reaction is incredulous laughter. | Most people just laugh with disbelief. |
| Bemused cynicism isn't the only response to the old system. | Some people respond to the old system by turning cynical; others say… |
| The current campus hostility is a symptom of the change. | It's easy to notice the change: you can see how angry all the students are. |
| We have a thunderstorm probability situation. | It may rain. |
| communication facilitation skills development intervention | a program to help students write better |

## A6. The breezy pig (voice)

**E. B. White** (formal grammar, plain words, a poet's cadence; it sounds effortless and isn't):
> I spent several days and nights in mid-September with an ailing pig and I feel driven to account for this stretch of time, more particularly since the pig died at last, and I lived, and things might easily have gone the other way round and none left to do the accounting.

**Zinsser's parody of the breezy style:**
> Ever stay up late babysitting for a sick porker? Believe you me, a guy can lose a heckuva lot of shut-eye. I did that gig for three nights back in September and my better half thought I'd lost my marbles. (Just kidding, Pam!) Frankly, the whole deal kind of bummed me out. Because, you see, the pig up and died on me. ...

[*Crude, corny, verbose, condescending ("you see"). Clichés ("lost my marbles", "kicked the bucket"), qualifiers ("kind of"), throat-clearing ("Frankly", "To tell you the truth"). The worst part: it's harder to read than White.*]

## A7. Verbless wonders (paragraphing)

**Before** (wire-service style, one fragment per paragraph):
> The No. 2 lawyer at the White House left work early on Tuesday, drove to an isolated park overlooking the Potomac River and took his life.
>
> A revolver in his hand, slumped against a Civil War-era cannon, he left behind no note, no explanation.
>
> Only friends, family and colleagues in stunned sorrow.
>
> And a life story that until Tuesday had read like any man's fantasy.

**After** (one paragraph, three real sentences):
> The No. 2 lawyer at the White House left work early on Tuesday, drove to an isolated park overlooking the Potomac River and took his life. A revolver in his hand, slumped against a Civil War-era cannon, he left behind no note, no explanation—only friends, family and colleagues in stunned sorrow. He also left behind a life story that until Tuesday had read like any man's fantasy.

**What changed:** the verbless fragments got verbs, and the train of thought became one logical unit. The first version calls out, "Yoo-hoo! Look how simple I'm making this for you!" The second gives the subject and the reader dignity.

## A8. The annotated paragraph (rewriting)

The full draft with Zinsser's bracketed notes, the revision, and the 13 techniques it demonstrates are in [methods.md](methods.md) under "Rewriting". The short version:

| Draft sentence | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "There used to be a time when neighbors took care of one another, he remembered." | Reflective tag at the end | Put "He remembered" first to set the tone. |
| "It no longer seemed to happen that way, however." | Contrast arrives last; locale unknown | "But that no longer seemed to happen in America." |
| "He wondered if it was because everyone in the modern world was so busy." | Same length and rhythm as the others | Make it a question: "Was it because everyone was so busy?" |
| "It occurred to him that people today have so many things to do that they don't have time for old-fashioned friendship." | Repeats the previous sentence | Warm it with specifics: "their television sets and their cars and their fitness programs". |
| "His friends had deserted him when he needed them most during his recent illness." | The strongest word is buried | End on it: "…was the time when he needed them most." |
| "It was almost as if they found him guilty of doing something shameful." | "Guilty" is implicit | Cut it. |
| "He recalled reading somewhere about societies in primitive parts of the world in which sick people were shunned…" | Sluggish; the irony is deferred | Break it up and snap off the point: "Or did it?" |

## A9. The Timbuktu word hunts (freshness)

The first two rows are Zinsser's own drafts. The third row's "first draft" is invented to show what he avoided.

| First draft | Final | How |
|---|---|---|
| "We were in our fifties and sixties." | "In years we ranged from late middle age to Medicare." | Kept looking for a proper name that brings a dull fact to life. |
| "Names like London and Paris didn't turn up in our accounts of earlier trips." | "Names like Venice and Versailles didn't bob up in our accounts of earlier trips, or even Marrakech or Luxor or Chiang Mai." | Tried capitals, then alliteration, then tourist cities, then tourist sites. Swapped "turn up" for the active, visual "bob". About an hour on one sentence. |
| "…we were the only tourists, which was amazing!" (a hypothetical draft) | "Mali got its independence in 1960. We were in Timbuktu for an event that hadn't been held in 27 years." | No comment and no exclamation point. Trust your material. |

## A10. The reference trap (flow)

**Before:**
> The tragic hero of the play is Othello. Small and malevolent, Iago feeds his jealous suspicions.

[*The name still in the reader's ear is Othello, so "small and malevolent" attaches to him.*]

**After** (the book diagnoses the problem without rewriting it; this is one fix):
> The tragic hero of the play is Othello. His jealous suspicions are fed by Iago, a small and malevolent man.

Or: *…is Othello. Iago, small and malevolent, feeds his jealous suspicions.* Always remember where you left the reader in the previous sentence.

# Part B: Applied to modern writing

## B1. README introduction

**Before:**
> Welcome to DataSync! DataSync is a robust, scalable, next-generation solution that leverages cutting-edge technologies to empower teams to seamlessly synchronize their data across a wide variety of heterogeneous environments. In this README, we will provide an overview of the various features and functionality, as well as instructions on how to get started utilizing the tool.

[*"Welcome to" is throat-clearing. "Robust, scalable, next-generation, cutting-edge, seamlessly" is fad words with no facts. "Leverages", "empower", "solution", "utilizing" are jargon. "In this README, we will provide…" announces instead of doing. The reader still doesn't know what it does.*]

**After:**
> DataSync copies tables between Postgres, MySQL and SQLite and keeps them in step. Point it at two databases, and it sends only the rows that changed, every few seconds or on demand.
>
> ```bash
> datasync --from postgres://prod --to sqlite://local.db
> ```

**Techniques:** lead with what it does, in one sentence. Specific nouns (Postgres, MySQL, rows) instead of "heterogeneous environments". Show, then tell. Cut the announcement.

## B2. Pull request description

**Before:**
> This PR implements a number of changes in order to facilitate the improvement of the overall performance of the search functionality. Basically, it should be noted that the previous implementation was sort of inefficient due to the fact that it was performing a full table scan on each and every request. Going forward, this will hopefully be significantly better.

[*Every clutter category at once. Inflated phrases: "in order to facilitate", "due to the fact that", "each and every", "going forward". Qualifiers: "basically", "sort of", "hopefully". Throat-clearing: "it should be noted that". A concept noun: "the improvement of the overall performance". No numbers. "Significantly better" is unproven.*]

**After:**
> Search was scanning the whole `products` table on every request. This adds an index on `(tenant_id, name)` and switches the query to use it.
>
> p95 latency on the staging dataset dropped from 1.8 s to 40 ms.
>
> Testing: ran the existing search suite and added a test that fails if the query plan uses a sequential scan.

**Techniques:** what was wrong, what changed, how we know. One thought per sentence. Real numbers instead of "significantly". No hedging. The reviewer can decide in ten seconds.

## B3. Incident email to customers

**Before:**
> Dear Valued Customer,
>
> We wanted to take this opportunity to reach out and inform you that on Tuesday, a service disruption was experienced by a subset of users due to an unforeseen issue with a third-party infrastructure provider. Our teams worked diligently around the clock to remediate the situation in a timely manner. We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused and remain committed to providing best-in-class reliability going forward.

[*"Valued Customer" is impersonal. "Take this opportunity to reach out and inform you" is three phrases of throat-clearing. "A service disruption was experienced" is a passive euphemism that hides what broke and who's responsible. "A subset of users": which? "Any inconvenience this may have caused" is a non-apology. "Best-in-class" is a fad phrase. No times, no impact, no next steps. Locate the missing "I".*]

**After:**
> Hi Ana,
>
> On Tuesday from 14:05 to 15:20 UTC, about 12% of customers, including your team, couldn't log in. Our login service depends on a single cloud region, and that region went down.
>
> We were too slow to switch to our backup region: it took us 50 minutes to notice that automatic failover hadn't started. That's on us, and I'm sorry.
>
> Two changes are already live: failover now starts automatically within two minutes, and we page an engineer if it doesn't. By the end of the month, login will run in two regions at once.
>
> If anything still looks wrong, reply to this email. It comes straight to me.
>
> Priya Shah, Head of Reliability

**Techniques:** a real person writing to a real person. Specific times and numbers. Active voice that owns the mistake ("we were too slow"). A plain apology. Concrete next steps. It stops when the job is done.

## B4. Weekly status update

**Before:**
> Hi all, just wanted to give a quick update on where things are at this week. Overall we've been making good progress on a number of fronts. The team has been heads-down working on the migration effort, and there have been some challenges, but we're working through them. We're aligned on next steps and will continue to circle back as needed. Let me know if you have any questions!

[*Four sentences and no information. "Just wanted to give a quick update" is throat-clearing. "Good progress on a number of fronts" is vague. "Some challenges" is a euphemism. "Aligned on next steps" and "circle back" are jargon. The exclamation point is gush. The reader can't make a single decision from this.*]

**After:**
> **Migration: 70% of accounts moved (last week 45%).** On track for June 14.
>
> **Blocked:** the billing export fails for accounts with more than 10,000 invoices. Lee is rewriting it to stream; expect a fix Thursday. If it slips, the June 14 date slips with it.
>
> **Need from you:** a decision on whether we email the 300 enterprise accounts before their move or after. I recommend before. Please reply by Wednesday.

**Techniques:** lead with the number that matters. Name the problem, the person and the date. Make the ask explicit, with a recommendation (take a stand). Short paragraphs with a bold label are a road map.

## B5. Product announcement

**Before:**
> We are incredibly excited to announce the launch of our revolutionary new AI-powered insights dashboard, which represents a paradigm shift in the way teams interact with their data. This game-changing feature empowers users to unlock actionable insights and drive meaningful outcomes like never before!

[*Every adjective is a claim with no proof: "incredibly excited", "revolutionary", "paradigm shift", "game-changing", "like never before". "Empowers users to unlock actionable insights and drive meaningful outcomes" contains no picture at all. The exclamation point tells readers how to feel. Overstatement destroys credibility.*]

**After:**
> You can now ask your dashboard questions in plain English. Type "Which regions missed quota last quarter?" and you'll get a chart and the query behind it, which you can edit.
>
> We built it because our own sales team was filing a dozen data requests a day. In a month of testing, those requests fell to two.

**Techniques:** show the thing doing something (a real question and a real result). Give the human reason it exists. A number that's modest and believable beats a superlative. Trust the reader to be impressed.

## B6. Technical explanation (upside-down pyramid)

**Before:**
> Eventual consistency is a consistency model used in distributed computing to achieve high availability that informally guarantees that, if no new updates are made to a given data item, eventually all accesses to that item will return the last updated value, which is in contrast to strong consistency models such as linearizability.

[*One sentence doing five jobs. It starts with the abstraction, not the reader's experience. It assumes the reader knows "linearizability". No example.*]

**After:**
> You change your profile photo, refresh the page, and still see the old one. A few seconds later, the new one appears.
>
> That's eventual consistency. Your photo is stored on several servers so the site stays up if one fails. When you update it, one server gets the change first and passes it along to the others. Until they all catch up, different servers can give different answers. Given a few seconds with no new changes, they agree.
>
> The trade-off: the site can keep working when servers fail or lose touch with each other, at the cost of sometimes showing stale data. Systems that need every reader to see the latest value at once, like bank balances, choose stronger consistency and accept that they may refuse requests during a failure.

**Techniques:** start with one narrow fact the reader has lived. Then the mechanism, one step per sentence. Then the broader significance. Assume the reader knows nothing; don't skip a step. Define by example before naming.

## B7. Feedback on a colleague's work (editing someone else)

**Before:**
> Overall this is a great doc! A few small nits: I think maybe the intro could potentially be a bit tighter? Also, not sure, but I feel like some sections might be slightly redundant. Just a thought though, feel free to ignore!

[*So hedged that the writer can't act on it. Six qualifiers in four sentences. Which sections? Redundant with what?*]

**After:**
> The proposal is convincing, and the cost table in section 3 will carry the meeting.
>
> Two changes would make it faster to read:
> 1. **Start with your recommendation.** Right now it's in the last paragraph. The first two paragraphs describe the history, which reviewers already know. You could cut them or move them to an appendix.
> 2. **Sections 4 and 6 both explain the migration plan.** Section 6 is clearer. I'd delete 4 and keep 6.
>
> I've bracketed a few phrases in the doc that I think can go without losing meaning. Your call on each.

**Techniques:** specific praise, specific problems, specific fixes. Name the problem (buried point, repetition) and offer the repair. Bracket rather than delete, and respect the writer's voice. Confident, not hedged.

## B8. A personal essay opening

**Before:**
> Ever since I was a young child, I have always been passionate about cooking. In today's fast-paced world, it is more important than ever to take time to slow down and appreciate the simple things in life. In this essay, I will explore what cooking means to me and why I believe it is so important.

[*A generic opener that could belong to anyone. A cliché ("fast-paced world", "simple things in life"). An announcement ("In this essay, I will explore"). Zinsser: the paragraphs most likely to be thrown away are the throat-clearing ones; start where you begin to sound like a person.*]

**After:**
> My grandmother measured flour with a chipped teacup and salt with the palm of her hand. When she died, I found the teacup in a drawer and tried to make her bread. It took me eleven loaves to get it right, and I'm still not sure about the salt.

**Techniques:** start in the action with one specific detail (the chipped teacup). Think small: one recipe, one person. The point arrives without being announced. The last line has a small twist that pulls the reader on.

## B9. Email that buries the request

**Before:**
> Hi Sam,
>
> Hope you had a great weekend! I wanted to follow up on our conversation from a couple of weeks ago regarding the Q3 planning process. As you may recall, we discussed the possibility of potentially bringing in additional resources to help support the analytics workstream, given the increased volume of requests that the team has been experiencing lately. I've been giving this some more thought and I think it might make sense to explore this further. Would you be open to discussing this at some point?

**After:**
> Hi Sam,
>
> Can I hire a contract analyst for Q3? Analytics requests have doubled since April (from 20 a week to 40), and we're now taking nine days to answer them. A contractor for three months would cost about $45,000 and would get us back to two days.
>
> If yes, I'll start interviews next week. Happy to talk it through on Thursday if that's easier.

**Techniques:** the request goes in the first sentence. Replace "increased volume" and "additional resources" with numbers and a noun (contract analyst). Give the cost and the payoff. Propose the next step.

## B10. Error message and UI copy

| Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| An unexpected error has occurred. Please try again later. | We couldn't save your changes because you're offline. They're stored on this device and will upload when you reconnect. | Say what happened, what it means and what happens next. |
| Authentication failure: invalid credentials provided. | That password doesn't match this email. Try again, or reset your password. | A person talking to "you"; offers a way out. |
| Are you sure you want to proceed with the deletion of this item? | Delete "Q3 budget.xlsx"? You can't undo this. | Name the thing; state the consequence; cut "proceed with the deletion of". |
| Your request is currently being processed. | Uploading… 3 of 12 files | Concrete progress beats a vague status. |
| Successfully completed the operation! | Saved. | Brevity; no gush. |

## How to use these examples

When revising, find the example closest to your situation and apply the same moves:

1. **Find the point** and put it first (B2, B4, B9).
2. **Bracket the clutter**: inflated phrases, qualifiers, throat-clearing, jargon (A3, B1, B2, B7).
3. **Locate the missing "I"** and get people doing things with active verbs (A1, A5, B3).
4. **Replace adjectives and claims with specifics**: numbers, names, a picture (B4, B5, B9).
5. **Order for the reader**: one thought per sentence, signal shifts early, end on the strong word (A8, A10, B6).
6. **Sound like one person** from start to finish, neither stiff nor breezy (A6, B3, B8).
7. **Stop** when the job is done.
