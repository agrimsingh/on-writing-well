## The Sound of Your Voice

### One voice for every subject
- Zinsser wrote one book about baseball and one about jazz, and wrote neither in "sports English" or "jazz English". Both were in the best English he could manage, in his usual style, so readers knew they were hearing from the same person.
- **Your commodity as a writer is you.** Don't alter your voice to fit your subject. Develop one voice readers recognize on the page, enjoyable for its musical line *and* for what it leaves out: **breeziness, condescension and clichés**.

### Breeziness vs. the effortless style
- The relaxed style that sounds like the author talking to you (E. B. White, Thurber, V. S. Pritchett, Lewis Thomas) is **not effortless**. It's achieved by strenuous effort and constant refining. "The nails of grammar and syntax are in place."
- White's opening: *"I spent several days and nights in mid-September with an ailing pig and I feel driven to account for this stretch of time, more particularly since the pig died at last, and I lived, and things might easily have gone the other way round and none left to do the accounting."* It sounds folksy, but nothing in it is accidental. The grammar is formal, the words plain and precise, the cadences a poet's. **The writer sounds confident; he isn't trying to ingratiate himself.**
- Inexperienced writers think a casual effect means being "just folks", a pal over the back fence. They're so eager not to seem formal that they don't try to write good English. Zinsser's parody of the same pig story in the breezy style:

  > Ever stay up late babysitting for a sick porker? Believe you me, a guy can lose a heckuva lot of shut-eye. I did that gig for three nights back in September and my better half thought I'd lost my marbles. (Just kidding, Pam!) Frankly, the whole deal kind of bummed me out. Because, you see, the pig up and died on me. ...

- What's wrong with it: crude, corny, verbose, contemptuous of the language, condescending. ("I stop reading writers who say 'You see.'") **Worst of all, it's harder to read than good English.** In trying to ease the reader's way, the writer has littered the path with cheap slang, shoddy sentences and windy philosophizing.
- The tools of grammar survived for centuries because readers need them and subconsciously want them. **Nobody ever stopped reading E. B. White because the writing was too good. Readers will stop reading you if they think you're talking down to them.**
- Write with respect for the language at its best and for readers at their best. If you're tempted by breeziness, read your draft aloud and see if you like the sound of your voice.

### Taste
- Taste is hard to define, but we know it when we meet it. **Knowing what not to do is a major part of taste.** The jazz pianist with taste puts every note to work; the one without it drenches us in ripples. The designer with taste knows less is more and that design serves the words.
- Taste shifts across decades, but every art has a core of verities that last (the Parthenon's proportions, Bach's fugues). Writing has few such guideposts, since it expresses each person's individuality. Still, **much can be gained by knowing what to omit.**
- **Clichés are the enemy of taste.** A writer who reaches for them unerringly picks the banal over the novel; "his voice is the voice of a hack." Clichés are everywhere, ready to help, and even careful writers use some in a first draft. Clean them out on rewrite. Listen for them when you read drafts aloud; notice how incriminating they sound.
- **Extend the point to all your language.** Taste picks words with surprise, strength and precision. Non-taste slips into the breezy vernacular of alumni-magazine class notes: "the top brass", "the powers that be". What's wrong with "top brass"? Nothing and everything. Call people what they are: officials, executives, chairmen, presidents, directors, managers. The corny synonym is also imprecise: *which* officers are the top brass? Non-taste uses "umpteenth", "zillions", and "Period." ("She said she didn't want to hear any more about it. Period.")
- Ultimately taste is an ear that hears the difference between a sentence that limps and one that lilts, and an intuition for when a casual phrase dropped into a formal sentence will seem the inevitable choice (White was a master of this balance).

### Imitation
- Perfect taste, like perfect pitch, is a gift, but a fair amount can be acquired. **Study writers who have it.**
- **Never hesitate to imitate another writer.** Imitation is part of learning any art. Bach and Picasso needed models. Find the best writers in the fields that interest you and **read their work aloud.** Get their voice, taste and attitude toward language into your ear. You won't lose your own identity. You'll shed those skins soon enough and become who you're supposed to become.
- Reading plugs you into a longer tradition and can tap a vein of eloquence or racial memory that gives your writing depth it couldn't reach alone.

### Eloquence
- Example: Connecticut Governor Wilbur Cross's 1936 Thanksgiving Proclamation (reissued in 1976 by Governor Ella Grasso as "a masterpiece of eloquence"): *"Time out of mind at this turn of the seasons when the hardy oak leaves rustle in the wind and the frost gives a tang to the air and the dusk falls early and the friendly evenings lengthen under the heel of Orion, it has seemed good to our people to join together in praising the Creator and Preserver, who has brought us by a way that we did not know to the end of another year..."*
- Some students thought Zinsser, the simplicity zealot, was posting it as florid excess. That raised the question: **what separates eloquence from bombast?** Why do Cross's words exalt us while most officials' "oratorical ruffles and flourishes" anesthetize us?
  1. **Taste.** The writer with an ear reaches for fresh imagery; the hack reaches for "tried and true" clichés.
  2. **Simplicity.** Enduring writing is made of **short, strong words**. Sedating words are three to five syllables, mostly Latin, often ending in "-ion", embodying a vague concept. Cross's proclamation has no four-syllable words and only ten three-syllable words (three of them proper nouns). Its words are concrete: *leaves, wind, frost, air, evening, earth, comforts, soil, labor, breath, body, justice, courage, peace, land, rites, home*. Homely in the best sense, and **all nouns. After verbs, plain nouns are your strongest tools; they resonate with emotion.**
  3. **What's left unsaid.** Eloquence moves us by touching off echoes of what we already know from our reading, religion and heritage. **It invites us to bring part of ourselves to the transaction.** Lincoln soaked himself in the King James Bible; the Second Inaugural's "It may seem strange that any men should dare to ask a just God's assistance in wringing their bread from the sweat of other men's faces, but let us judge not, that we be not judged" borrows from Genesis, Matthew and Isaiah. Cross doesn't explain "the long search after truth" or "liberty and justice freely granted". We supply our own experience, and he doesn't waste our time. A hack would marshal clichés to "tell us far more — and nourish us far less."
- **Remember the uses of the past.** What moves us in writing with regional or ethnic roots (Southern, African-American, Jewish-American) is the sound of voices older than the narrator's. Toni Morrison: "I remember the language of the people I grew up with... when you have something important to say you go into parable... It was the style... I couldn't describe it, but I could produce it."
- **Go with what seems inevitable in your own heritage. Embrace it and it may lead you to eloquence.**

## Enjoyment, Fear and Confidence

### Enjoyment
- The old *New York Herald Tribune* conveyed enjoyment: everyone on it seemed to be having a wonderful time, and articles carried an extra touch of grace, humanity or humor, "some gift of themselves" to readers. (John O'Reilly's annual mock-scientific dispatch on the woolly bear caterpillar's winter forecast ran on page one. The point was to give everybody a good time.)
- **A sense of enjoyment is a priceless attribute for a writer.** Writing is lonely work, so keep yourself cheered up. **If something strikes you as funny while writing, put it in to amuse yourself.** If you think it's funny, a few others will too. Don't worry about the readers who won't be amused.
- S. J. Perelman on comic writing: "It takes audacity and exuberance and gaiety, and the most important one is audacity." Then: **"The reader has to feel that the writer is feeling good."** Then: **"Even if he isn't."** Perelman's life held more than its share of depression, yet he went to his typewriter daily "and made the English language dance".
- **Writers have to jump-start themselves at the moment of performance**, like actors, dancers and musicians. The writers who sweep us along (Mailer, Wolfe, Morrison, Buckley, Thompson, David Foster Wallace) make an effort every morning to turn on the switch. **You also have to turn on the switch. Nobody is going to do it for you.**

### Fear
- Fear of writing is planted early, usually at school, and never fully leaves. The blank page or screen can freeze you into writing nothing, or writing words less than wonderful.
- Approach writing as a chore and you'll get sludge. The consolation: **you get another shot at those dismal sentences tomorrow, and the day after.** With each rewrite, force your personality onto the material.
- **The nonfiction writer's biggest fear: not bringing off the assignment.** Fiction writers invent their world, so we can only say "It doesn't work for me." Nonfiction writers are **infinitely accountable**: to the facts, to the people interviewed, to the place and events, and to the craft and its perils (losing, confusing or boring the reader). With every inaccuracy or misstep of craft, we can say, "That's wrong."

### Building confidence
1. **Write about subjects that interest you and that you care about.** Allen Ginsberg's psychiatrist, asked what the professional association would say about him quitting market research for poetry: **"There's no party line."** You can be your own party line.
2. **"Living is the trick."** (Red Smith, eulogizing a fellow sportswriter.) Smith wrote about sports for 55 years without succumbing to the pressure to write about something "serious", and said more about American values than many writers who were so serious nobody could read them. **Writers who write interestingly keep themselves interested.** Use writing to give yourself an interesting life and a continuing education. Write about what you'd enjoy knowing about; the enjoyment will show. Learning is a tonic.
3. **Sincerity is a credential.** You'll be thrown into specialized worlds and worry you aren't qualified. Zinsser had never done sports reporting when he went to spring training; nobody asked "What else have you written about baseball?" because it was obvious he really wanted to know how they did their work. **Your best credential is yourself.**
4. **Think broadly about the assignment.** It may touch an unexpected corner of your experience. Zinsser turned down an *Audubon* piece (he can identify only the pigeon), then saw a film about Roger Tory Peterson still working full tilt at 84. Birds weren't his subject, but survivors were. The "informal visit" became a 4,000-word profile: once he saw Peterson's studio he realized Peterson was above all an artist, and the story became an art story, a teaching story and a survivor story as well as a bird story. Don't assume an *Audubon* article must be strictly about nature. **Push the boundaries; bring some part of your own life to it. It's not your version of the story until you write it.**
5. **Write about people you respect.** Zinsser's profiles were nearly all of people whose work he knew and loved, and his gratitude was a source of energy. Writing to destroy or scandalize can be as destructive to the writer as to the subject.
6. **Master the tools.** If you master interviewing and orderly construction, and bring your general intelligence and humanity, **you can write about any subject.** (The *Audubon* piece was received by birders as the definitive Peterson portrait, prompting a phone message: "Is this the William Zinsser who writes about nature?")

### Interviewing experts
- The expert knows so much because it's their field. You're a generalist making their work accessible, which means **prodding them to clarify what seems obvious to them.**
- **Trust your common sense about what you need to know, and don't be afraid to ask a dumb question. If the expert thinks you're dumb, that's his problem.**
- **Test: is the expert's first answer sufficient? Usually not.** Peterson, taciturn, would answer and stop. Zinsser kept asking: how does your technique differ now? Why add more detail now? "Many people think good equipment makes it easy... the camera does it all." Why doesn't it? **Press with "Why not?" and "What else?"** He got three answers (eye and composition; the quality of light; knowledge of the animal, such as anticipating feeding frenzies). "So we proceeded, Mr. Expert and Mr. Stupid."
- **"That's interesting."** When you catch yourself saying it, pay attention and follow your nose. **Trust your curiosity to connect with the curiosity of your readers.** (Peterson: "The attitude of people towards birds has changed the attitude of birds towards people." Mockingbirds love shopping malls, "they sit there and direct the traffic.")
- **"Have I seen everything?"** The best material often comes after the pencil is put away, in the chitchat of leave-taking, when the subject, off the hook, offers afterthoughts. That question got Zinsser into Peterson's cellar of 2,000 specimens, including an acorn woodpecker tagged 1882.
- Write a story once, as well as you can. Zinsser wouldn't rewrite the same story twice; he let the publisher reprint his article and wrote 4,000 new words on a new angle, method and process, for a new audience.

## The Tyranny of the Final Product

### Process over product
- Students who say "I have an idea that would be perfect for *Sports Illustrated*" are already picturing the headline, layout and byline. **Fixation on the finished article deflects writers from the earlier decisions that determine its shape, voice and content.**
- Ours is a culture that worships the winning result. Less glamorous gains (learning, wisdom, growth, confidence, dealing with failure) can't be graded, so they get less respect. For writers, the grade is the check.
- Zinsser won't answer "How can I sell my writing?" He wants to teach people to write. **If the process is sound, the product will take care of itself.**

### Organizing long pieces: the most untaught skill
- Writers are endlessly taught the clear declarative sentence. But ask for something longer and "the sentences leach out all over the floor like marbles." Every editor knows that moment of irreversible chaos. **The writer, eye on the finish line, never gave enough thought to how to run the race.**
- Zinsser's experiment: a "People and Places" course in which **no writing is required**. Each student describes one place they'd like to write about, why and how, and the class thinks its way to solutions. Thoughts haven't been committed to sacred paper; they can be changed, rearranged or disowned.
- **Go deeper; find your connection to the place.** A woman whose church had burned proposed interviewing the minister, organist, firemen, sexton and choirmaster. "You've given us five good pieces by [a competent newspaper reporter]. But they're not good enough for you." Sit in church for a few Sundays and let it tell you what the fire means.

### The recurring problems
- **Compression** (the biggest): distilling a coherent narrative from a tangle of facts, feelings and memories. "The disappearance of small towns in Iowa" would be all generalization and no humanity. Write about **one** town, and within it one store, one family or one farmer. **Think the story down to human scale.**
- **Focus:** of all the stories in this place, which is *yours*? A woman returning to empty her late mother's house planned to interview her ten siblings. Was it their story? No, hers. Then interviewing them would be almost a complete waste of time.
- **Voice and tense:** writing about a childhood school, should she write as the girl or as the woman today? Whose truth is the "right" truth? There's no one answer, **but it must be settled in advance. Readers won't put up with vacillating between two perspectives and tones.**
- **Time to find the story.** A student would try X, Y, Z, P, Q and R (all dull, even to him) and then stumble on M, a forgotten, seemingly minor but unassailably true memory that compressed everything that made him want to write about the town. "There's your story." He had been given time to find it.
- **Too many stories under one roof.** A lawyer wanted to write about boyhood soccer friendships (memoir), the same friends bonded in midlife (personal essay), and the decline of the sport's texture today (investigative reporting). Each was a good subject; together they'd drive him crazy. He did eventually fit them under one roof, but the house had to be greatly enlarged, and it took three years. Even then the manuscript was "still one rewrite away".

### Writing beyond publication
- Freedom from the deadline is liberating. **The right to fail is as liberating as the right to succeed.**
- Many good reasons to write have nothing to do with publishing. **Writing for yourself is a powerful search mechanism: there's no better way to find out who you are, what you know and what you think.** Family histories and memoirs written for children and grandchildren are satisfying, and often charming as social history.
- Allow as much time as the journey needs.

### Quest and intention
- **Quest:** one of the oldest themes in storytelling, an act of faith readers never tire of. Students who used a place to search for something deeper (a meaning, an idea, a sliver of the past) found an echo in everyone else's yearnings. **Any time you can tell a story as a quest or pilgrimage, you're ahead of the game. Readers bring their own associations and do some of your work for you.**
- **Intention:** what you wish to accomplish, "the writer's soul". You can write to affirm and celebrate or to debunk and destroy; the choice is yours. Nobody can make you write what you don't want to write. You aren't required to carry the trash for editors with a commercial agenda.
- **Writing is related to character. If your values are sound, your writing will be sound.** Figure out what you want to do and how, and work your way with humanity and integrity to the finished piece.

## A Writer's Decisions

Writing is countless successive decisions, some big ("What should I write about?") and some as small as the smallest word. All of them matter. This chapter covers the little decisions of organizing a long piece, using Zinsser's annotated travel article "The News From Timbuktu" (*Condé Nast Traveler*).

### What holds a long piece together
- **Writing is linear and sequential. Logic is the glue. Tension must be maintained** from sentence to sentence, paragraph to paragraph and section to section. **Narrative (good old storytelling) should pull readers along without their noticing the tug.** All they should notice is that you've made a sensible plan. **Every step should seem inevitable.**
- Organizing a long article is as important as writing a clear, pleasing sentence; those sentences fall apart without it.

### The lead (six paragraphs, as long as the rest of the piece took to write)
- The hardest decision is how to begin. The lead grabs with a provocative idea, then each paragraph holds the reader while adding information, so readers will stay for the whole trip. **It can be one paragraph or as long as it needs to be. It's over when the necessary work is done and you can relax into the narrative.**
- **Paragraph 1: an arresting idea the reader has never thought of.** *"What struck me most powerfully when I got to Timbuktu was that the streets were of sand. I suddenly realized that sand is very different from dirt. Every town starts with dirt streets that eventually get paved as the inhabitants prosper and subdue their environment. But sand represents defeat. A city with streets of sand is a city at the edge."* Five plain declarative sentences, no commas, **one thought each**. Readers process one idea at a time, in sequence. Much trouble comes from making one sentence do too much. **Never be afraid to break a long sentence into two or three.**
- **Paragraph 2: grow its first sentence out of the previous last sentence** ("That, of course, is why I was there: Timbuktu is the ultimate destination for edge-seekers"), so the reader has no chance to squirm away. Then **acknowledge what the reader already knows or half knows** (Timbuktu as a word for the unreachable, a songwriter's "oo" rhyme). This welcomes the reader as a fellow traveler and adds enjoyable lore rather than hard facts.
- **Paragraph 3: hard work that can't be put off.** Three sentences crammed with history (the explorers, Leo Africanus's city of 100,000, the salt and gold trade). **That's all a magazine reader needs. Don't give readers more than they require; if you want to tell more, write a book.**
- **Paragraph 4: ask "What does the reader want to know next?" after every sentence.** Here: why did *I* go? It also **establishes the writer's personality and voice.** In travel writing you are the guide; take readers on *your* trip so they identify with your hopes and apprehensions. "Bright enough or dumb enough" evokes the tourist as patsy; "to beat the crowd" was put in to amuse himself. (He admits it's late to say where Timbuktu is, but he couldn't place it earlier without pulling apart the lead.)
- **Paragraph 5: let other people do the work in their own words**, which are usually more revealing than yours. The tour brochure's gushing promise ("Hundreds of camels... triumphant entry... Spend the night in a desert tent as guest of the tribal chief") sets up the expectation and is itself a window into the promoters' grandiosity. **Watch for funny or self-serving quotes and use them with gratitude.**
- **Paragraph 6:** introduces the companions and closes the lead. Once it was wrestled into place, he felt "confidently launched" and trusted that readers still with him would stay to the end.

### Word hunts: banality is the enemy
- **The challenge is not to write like everybody else.** "We were in our fifties and sixties" was serviceable, "but the merely serviceable is a drag." A merciful muse produced **"from late middle age to Medicare."** **Look long enough and you'll usually find a proper name or metaphor that brings a dull but necessary fact to life.**
- "Names like London and Paris didn't turn up in our accounts of earlier trips" took almost an hour to fix. He tried other capitals (no better), alliteration (Madrid and Moscow? too tricky), tourist-infested cities (Venice; but Vienna was too close), then tourist sites, and hit **Versailles**. "Turn up" became **"bob"**, an active three-letter verb that paints an object rising to the surface. Luxor, Marrakech and Chiang Mai, exotic in the 1950s, became the "commonplace" places. **No writing decision is too small to be worth a large expenditure of time.** Both you and the reader know when finicky labor is rewarded.
- "Tremor" and "forsaken" took time; "forsaken" came from Roget's, a word he'd never used, and as one of Jesus's last words it carries maximum loneliness. "Canons", "brazenly", "gaudy", "tendered": **vivid and precise, but not long or fancy, and words readers weren't expecting and therefore welcome.**
- "Aloneness" isn't his kind of word (too "poetic"), but no other word did the job, so he reluctantly kept it. **Use the word that does the job, even when it isn't your favorite.**

### Structure markers
- **The asterisk (or white space) is a signpost.** It tells readers a new phase begins: a change of chronology (a flashback), subject, emphasis or tone. After a compressed lead, it lets the writer "take a deep breath and start over" at a storyteller's gait.
- It also **takes anxiety out of writing**: you break the material into manageable chunks and take one at a time. The task seems less formidable, and panic is staved off.

### What is it *really* about?
- Mali got four quick paragraphs, despite libraries of scholarship on the Dogon and Niger peoples, because **this wasn't an article about Mali; it was about a quest for a camel caravan.** Decision: get across Mali as fast as possible, in the barest sentences about the route and what mattered.
- Ask **"What is the piece *really* about?"**, not just "What is it about?" **Fondness for material you worked hard to gather isn't a reason to include it if it isn't central.** Self-discipline "bordering on masochism" is required.
- Consolation: cut material isn't totally lost. It remains as an intangible the reader senses. **Readers should always feel you know more about your subject than you've put in writing.**

### Persona and humor
- Tiny jokes keep the writer amused and **maintain a persona**. "Even now tribal chiefs were turning their thoughts to how to entertain me in their desert tent." **The credulous narrator** is one of the oldest strains in travel and humor writing; used in moderation, making yourself gullible gives readers the pleasure of feeling superior.
- **Snappers:** small jokes at the end of a paragraph (the chieftain's tent "he didn't say anything about"; apprentices, "Nobody said what they were apprenticing for") propel readers into the next paragraph and keep them in a good mood.
- The hardest sentences are often the ones meant to be as simple as the rest **yet carry a small surprise** (the drivers spending the whole siesta "seeming to dismantle the engine").

### Resonance
- References to *Beau Geste*, *The Four Feathers* and the British "desert eccentrics" (Doughty, Burton, T. E. Lawrence, Thesiger) tap associations readers bring. Hollywood made much of Timbuktu's legend. **Resonance can do a great deal of emotional work that writers can't do on their own.** It also tells readers about the emotional baggage the writer carries, which they're entitled to know.

### Trust your material
- *"Mali got its independence in 1960. We were in Timbuktu for an event that hadn't been held in 27 years."* A small bomb, allowed to speak for itself, "just the facts, please", **with no comment and no exclamation point**. Notifying readers that it's amazing would spoil their pleasure of discovery.
- Another startling fact that needs no embellishment: one Land Rover having its tire pumped by a boy with a bicycle pump before heading into the Sahara.
- Once the story is moving, **settle into straight narrative**: one declarative sentence after another, told as simply as possible.

### Ending: let the story tell you
- He assumed he'd finish the trade cycle: back to Timbuktu, the salt unloaded, bought and sold. But the nearer he got, the less he wanted to write it. It loomed as drudgery for him and the reader.
- **You're under no obligation to the actual shape of events.** The real climax wasn't finding the caravan; it was the nomad family with almost nothing offering to share their dinner, the timeless hospitality and "nobility of living on the edge" he'd come to find.
- **When your story tells you it's over, regardless of what happened next, look for the door.** Before leaving, check the unities: the writer-guide who started the trip is the one ending it. The closing line ("I was too busy dreaming that I was Lawrence of Arabia") preserves the persona, gathers the associations and brings the journey full circle.

### Make your own luck
- **"Get on the plane."** Zinsser's best pieces (jazz introduced to China in Shanghai; Gregorian chants on a French horn in St. Mark's at night) came from going along with no assurance anything would happen. Same with Timbuktu, spring training and *Writing to Learn*.
- Nervousness is part of the deal; **a little nervousness gives writing an edge.** If a subject interests you, go after it. It won't come looking for you.
- **"Decide what you want to do. Then decide to do it. Then do it."**

## Write as Well as You Can

### Standards inherited
- From his mother: **good writing can appear anywhere**, even in the lowly newspaper. What matters is the writing, not the medium. So **write as well as you can by your own standards; never change your style to fit the size or presumed education of your audience.** Humor and optimism are lubricants in writing as in life.
- From his father, a shellac manufacturer who treated business as an art, used only the best ingredients and never went into a store looking for a bargain: **a bone-deep belief that quality is its own reward.** When endlessly rewriting what he'd endlessly rewritten, the inner voice Zinsser heard "was the voice of my father talking about shellac."

### Part entertainer
- **Think of yourself as part entertainer.** Writers dislike the word, but your piece must jump off the page by being more diverting than everyone else's.
- Usually that means **giving the reader an enjoyable surprise**: humor, anecdote, paradox, an unexpected quotation, a powerful fact, an outlandish detail, a circuitous approach, an elegant arrangement of words. **These become your "style." Liking a writer's style means liking their personality on paper.** A writer asks us to travel with them, and we pick the companion who'll try to brighten the trip.

### There are no new discoveries
- No breakthrough in writing a clear sentence is coming; that's been known since the King James Bible. **Verbs have more vigor than nouns; active verbs beat passive; short words and sentences are easier to read than long; concrete details are easier to process than vague abstractions.**
- The rules get bent (Victorian ornament; Tom Wolfe's headlong exuberance), but such acrobats are rare. **Most nonfiction writers do well to cling to the ropes of simplicity and clarity.** We all work with the same words and principles.

### Where the edge is
- **Ninety percent is the hard work of mastering the tools** in this book. Add a few points for a musical ear, a sense of rhythm and a feeling for words.
- The final advantage is the same as in any competitive venture: **to write better than everybody else, you have to want to.** Take an obsessive pride in the smallest details of your craft.
- **Be willing to defend what you've written** against middlemen whose sights and standards may differ. Too many writers are browbeaten into settling for less than their best.
- Style, "the careful projection onto paper of who I think I am", is your main marketable asset. Protect it. Zinsser fights over every semicolon. **To defend what you've written is a sign that you are alive.** His crankiness brought more work than it drove away, because editors knew he'd take unusual care.
- **Be reliable.** The craft involves more than writing: deliver on time and accurately. Editors properly drop writers they can't count on.

### Good editors
- Good editors bring **an objective eye the writer has long since lost**. They prune, shape and clarify; tidy inconsistencies of tense, pronoun, location and tone; catch sentences that could be read two ways; split awkward long sentences; put the writer back on the main road; build bridges where transitions failed; question judgment and taste; change focus or emphasis; suggest a different lead; cut excess. (Zinsser twice threw out a whole chapter on an editor's advice.)
- Above all, good editors are **generous**: enthusiastic about the shared project and confident the writer can make it work.
- **An editor's hand must be invisible.** Whatever an editor adds should sound like the writer's words.
- A good editor likes nothing better than copy he hardly has to touch.

### Bad editors
- Damage comes in two forms: **altering style and altering content.**
- **Style:** the bad editor has a compulsion to tinker, proving with busywork that he knows the minutiae of grammar. "Catching cracks in the road but not enjoying the scenery." It doesn't occur to him that **the writer is writing by ear**, after a particular sound or cadence, or playing with words. Example: "They don't look like cities that get visited by many visiting artists" was "corrected" to "...that are on the itinerary of many visiting artists." **The repetition was deliberate**: it surprises and refreshes readers in midsentence. The editor applied the rule about substituting synonyms. One such erosion leads to another; take a stand. "If you allow your distinctiveness to be edited out, you will lose one of your main virtues. You will also lose your virtue."
- **Content: the cardinal sin is tampering with a writer's opinions** ("They had written a whole new lead and had me saying things that aren't what I believe"). Editors will do what writers allow, especially on deadline. With every surrender, writers teach editors they can be treated like hired help.

### Negotiation and trust
- The ideal is **negotiation and trust**. If an editor's clarification loses a fact or nuance the writer included for reasons the editor didn't know, the writer should ask for the point back, and the editor should oblige while insisting on fixing what was unclear.
- **Clarity is what every editor owes the reader. Never let anything into print that you don't understand. If you don't, at least one other person won't, and that's one too many.**
- Writer and editor go through the manuscript together, finding for every problem the solution that best serves the finished piece. It works as well by phone. Don't accept distance or disarray ("we were on deadline", "it got put in the wrong pile") as excuses for changing work without consent.
- **Courtesies are organic to the craft**: acknowledge receipt, read with reasonable speed, say whether it's OK, return it promptly if not, work supportively on changes, send proofs, pay promptly. They're the code of honor that anchors the enterprise.

### Your work is yours
- **The purposes writers serve must be their own.** What you write is yours and nobody else's. Take your talent as far as you can and guard it with your life. Only you know how far that is.
- **Writing well means believing in your writing and yourself, taking risks, daring to be different, pushing yourself to excel. You will write only as well as you make yourself write.**
- Joe DiMaggio, who made the hardest catch look effortless through great daily effort, on why he played so well so consistently: **"I always thought that there was at least one person in the stands who had never seen me play, and I didn't want to let him down."** That's the definition of a careful writer.

## Applying Part IV: a self-check

```
- [ ] Does it sound like one consistent person, and like me, not like the subject's jargon?
- [ ] Any breeziness ("you see", slang, forced chumminess) or condescension? Read it aloud.
- [ ] Clichés and corny synonyms replaced with fresh, precise words?
- [ ] Do I sound like I'm enjoying this? Did I keep the thing that amused me?
- [ ] Did I write about what interests me, and think broadly about the assignment?
- [ ] Did I accept any first answer that wasn't sufficient? Did I ask the dumb question?
- [ ] Is it compressed to human scale? Is it MY story? Voice and tense settled?
- [ ] What is it REALLY about? Is anything in only because I worked hard to get it?
- [ ] Are sections signposted? Does each paragraph pull from the last?
- [ ] Did a dull necessary fact get a fresh proper name or metaphor?
- [ ] Did I trust my material, with no labels or exclamation points on the surprising facts?
- [ ] Did I stop where the story told me to, with the unities intact?
- [ ] Is it as good as I can make it, for the reader who has never seen me play?
```
