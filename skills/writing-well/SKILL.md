# Writing Well

Distilled from William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (25th anniversary edition). The book's four articles of faith are **clarity, simplicity, brevity and humanity**. Everything below serves one of them.

## Core beliefs

1. **Writing is thinking on paper.** Clear thinking becomes clear writing; a muddy thinker can't write good English for long. Before and after every sentence ask: *What am I trying to say?* then *Have I said it, clearly, to someone meeting the subject for the first time?*
2. **Rewriting is the essence of writing.** Very few sentences come out right the first, third or fifth time. The game is won or lost in revision. If writing feels hard, that's because it is hard.
3. **If the reader is lost, the writer was careless.** The reader has a short attention span and many rivals for it, and will blame themselves only briefly before leaving for good.
4. **Clutter is the disease.** Strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Writing improves in direct ratio to what you keep out of it that shouldn't be there.
5. **The product is the writer.** Readers stay for a person, not a topic. Humanity and warmth come from the person behind the words, not from gimmicks.
6. **Craft vs. attitude.** Be meticulous about craft: never lose a reader through sloppiness. Be carefree about whether the reader likes you or agrees. Write for yourself, and you'll reach the readers worth reaching.
7. **Be yourself.** Style is organic, not something bought and draped on. Never write what you wouldn't comfortably say in conversation.

## Workflow

Copy this checklist and work through it:

```
- [ ] 1. Decide the unities and the one point
- [ ] 2. Write a lead that hooks and then does work
- [ ] 3. Draft the body: linear, specific, human
- [ ] 4. Stop at the right place; no summary
- [ ] 5. Rewrite in passes (clutter, words, sentences, flow, sound, voice)
- [ ] 6. Final checks (accuracy, fairness, "Do I understand every sentence?")
```

### 1. Decide before writing

Settle these questions first. Readers need unity to trust that someone is at the helm:

- **Capacity:** reporter, explainer, participant, ordinary person?
- **Pronoun:** first person (participant) or third (observer)? Don't switch.
- **Tense:** choose the principal tense. Glances backward or forward are fine; drifting isn't.
- **Mood and style:** impersonal reportorial, personal but formal, or personal and casual. Any tone works. Mixing tones doesn't.
- **Attitude:** involved, detached, judgmental, ironic, amused?
- **Scope:** *think small.* Nobody can write "about" a big subject. Bite off one corner and cover it well (one man pursuing one whale). There is no last word, so drop the urge to be definitive.
- **The one point:** what single provocative thought should the reader take away? Not two, not five. The point also shapes the tone: earnest, dry understatement or humor.
- **What is it *really* about?** Material you worked hard to gather still goes if it isn't central.

If the material pulls you somewhere better while drafting, follow it. Don't be a prisoner of your plan. Afterward, rewrite the beginning so the whole piece is consistent.

### 2. The lead

- The first sentence is the most important. Each sentence must pull the reader into the next.
- **Hook** with freshness, novelty, paradox, humor, surprise, an unusual idea, an interesting fact or a question. **Then do work:** give hard details showing why the piece exists and why it's worth reading. Keep the reader curious.
- A story is the oldest hook. Start in the action, not with the alarm clock ("breakfast-to-bed").
- An odd, specific fact often saves a lead better than style does. Gather more material than you'll use.
- Retire stock leads: the future archaeologist, the visitor from Mars, the cute event "one day not long ago", and the "what do X, Y and Z have in common?" opener.
- The opening paragraphs most likely to be thrown away are the generic, throat-clearing ones. Start where you begin to sound like a person.

### 3. The body

- **Linear and sequential.** One thought per sentence. Each sentence follows from the one before; each paragraph amplifies the last. After every sentence ask: *What does the reader need to know next?*
- **Keep readers oriented.** Signal every shift of time, place, subject or mood *early* (but, yet, however, meanwhile, now, later, today, instead, still, thus).
- **End paragraphs with a springboard**: a small twist, surprise or "snapper" that carries the reader into the next one.
- **Specific over general.** Concrete details, numbers, names and images do the persuading. Choose the significant detail, not the known attribute.
- **People.** Find the human element in any subject. Let people speak in their own words. Get people doing things with real verbs.
- **Trust your material.** Present the colorful fact; color is part of the fact. Don't pre-label facts ("surprisingly", "of course"), and don't over-explain what readers can work out.
- **Credibility is fragile.** Never inflate. One bogus statement makes everything suspect.
- Use asterisks or white space to mark a change of chronology, subject or tone in long pieces. They help readers follow the map and help you write one chunk at a time.

### 4. The ending

- **When you're ready to stop, stop.** Once the facts are presented and the point is made, look for the nearest exit.
- Don't summarize. "In sum…" and "What insights can we glean…?" are the sound of cranking, and readers quit.
- The best ending slightly surprises yet feels exactly right, like a curtain line. Good options: a quotation with finality, humor or an unexpected closing detail; an echo of the opening (full circle); a short sentence that jolts with its fitness.
- Often the material tells you where it's over, even if events continued. Believe it.

### 5. Rewrite in passes

Rewriting mostly means reshaping, tightening and refining the raw material, not drafting new versions. Do these passes:

1. **Clutter pass (the brackets).** Bracket everything that isn't doing useful work: unnecessary prepositions on verbs ("head up", "free up"), adverbs that repeat the verb ("blared loudly"), adjectives stating known facts ("tall skyscraper"), little qualifiers ("sort of", "a bit", "rather", "very"), empty phrases ("in a sense"), and whole sentences that repeat or tell readers what they already know or can work out. Read without the brackets. **Most first drafts can lose about half their length without losing information or voice.**
2. **Word pass.** Short word over long ("help" not "assistance"). Plain over pompous. Precise over approximate. No jargon, journalese, fad words or clichés. Concrete nouns and strong, active, specific verbs. See [clutter-and-usage.md](clutter-and-usage.md).
3. **Sentence pass.** Active voice unless there's no comfortable alternative. Break long sentences. Turn concept nouns back into people doing things. Put the strongest word last. Vary length and order so sentences don't all move at the same plodding gait. A short sentence carries a punch.
4. **Flow pass.** Read sequentially and remember where you left the reader. Check for missing links between sentence A and sentence B, unflagged shifts, ambiguous references (the last name mentioned is the one the reader attaches the next description to), things told twice, and facts withheld too long.
5. **Sound pass.** Read it aloud. Readers hear what they read, so rhythm, cadence and alliteration matter. Fix what limps.
6. **Voice pass.** Does it sound like a person, and like *you*? Remove airs, hedges and breeziness. Use contractions where they fit. Make sure the tone holds from start to finish.

**The quickest fix:** when a phrase resists every rearrangement, ask "Do I need it at all?" Usually not. Delete it.

### 6. Final checks

- Never let anything out that you don't understand yourself.
- Quotes are accurate in words and context, and fair to the speaker (see [forms.md](forms.md)).
- Take a stand: no "it remains to be seen" or "it is too early to tell". Opinions are stated firmly, without last-minute escapes.
- The piece leaves one clear point and stops.

## Sentence-level rules at a glance

| Area | Rule |
|------|------|
| Verbs | The most important tool. Active, precise and vivid. Avoid verbs that need a tacked-on preposition, and vague ones like "stepped down" (resigned? retired? fired?). |
| Nouns | After verbs, plain concrete nouns are strongest. Avoid concept nouns ("reaction", "utilization") and noun stacks ("communication facilitation skills development intervention"). |
| Adverbs | Mostly unnecessary. Drop any that repeat the verb. Retire "decidedly", "arguably", "eminently", "virtually". |
| Adjectives | Mostly unnecessary. Use one only when it does work the noun can't ("garish daffodils", "red dirt"). No adjective-by-habit ("gnarled oak"). |
| Qualifiers | Prune "a bit", "sort of", "kind of", "rather", "quite", "very", "too", "pretty much", "in a sense". Each one whittles away the reader's trust. Don't be kind of bold. Be bold. |
| Period | Most writers don't reach it soon enough. Split sentences that carry two thoughts. |
| Exclamation point | Avoid. Let word order create emphasis. Never use it to flag a joke. |
| Semicolon | Use sparingly; it slows the pace. Prefer the period and the dash. |
| Dash | Fully respectable. A single dash amplifies or justifies; a pair sets off an aside. |
| Colon | Brings the sentence to a halt before a list or an example. |
| Mood changers | Signal shifts at the *start*. "But" is the strongest opener. Put "however" early in the sentence but not first, and never last. |
| Contractions | Use them for warmth ("I'll", "won't", "can't"). Avoid "I'd/he'd" (had or would?) and invented ones ("could've"). |
| That / which | Default to "that". Use ", which" for a nonrestrictive clause that describes, locates or explains something already identified. |
| Paragraphs | Keep them short; writing is visual. But don't chop a train of thought into one-line fragments. Think in paragraph units. |
| Pronouns | Avoid the generic "he". Recast with plurals, "we", "you", "our/the" or general nouns. Use "he or she" sparingly. Never "he/she". |
| Repetition | Repeating the clearest word ("the batter", "the runner") beats elegant variation. Don't recycle a person into five epithets. |
| Numbers | Report the ones that matter; don't run a statistics dump. |
| Overstatement | Don't. Let the humor sneak up. |

## Voice and attitude

- **No breeziness or condescension.** The "effortless" style takes strenuous effort, and its grammar is formal and exact. Cheap slang, "you see", forced chumminess and verbless fragments are harder to read and talk down to readers.
- **Taste is mostly knowing what to omit**: clichés, corny synonyms ("top brass"), "zillions", ornament that does no work.
- **Eloquence** comes from short, strong, concrete words and from echoes of what readers already carry (scripture, history, heritage). It leaves things unsaid and invites the reader to bring something to the transaction.
- **Confidence:** write about what interests you; sincerity is your best credential. Don't be afraid to ask the dumb question; check whether the expert's first answer was enough.
- **Enjoyment shows.** The reader has to feel the writer is feeling good. If something amuses you, put it in.
- **Intention:** write to affirm rather than to destroy. Writing is tied to character.
- **Write as well as you can**, for the one reader who has never seen you play.

## Editing someone else's writing

- Use the bracket mindset: *"I think this can go without losing meaning. You decide."* Don't violate the writer's voice.
- An editor's hand must be invisible. Anything you add should sound like the writer.
- Clarify every sentence you don't understand. If you don't understand it, other readers won't either.
- Never change opinions or content to suit yourself. Don't tinker for its own sake, and don't "fix" deliberate repetition or rhythm.
- Show the reasoning: name the problem (clutter, missing transition, buried point, tense shift) and offer the repair.

## Applying this to everyday technical and business writing

Zinsser's business chapter applies directly to docs, READMEs, PR descriptions, incident reports, status updates and email:

- Lead with what the reader most needs: what happened, what changed, what to do.
- Write like a person, not an institution. Use "we" and "I", active verbs, and real people doing real things.
- Replace jargon and acronyms with plain words, or define them once. Translate abstractions into things readers can picture.
- Explain processes as an upside-down pyramid. Start with the one narrow fact the reader needs first, then broaden toward significance and implications. Assume the reader knows nothing, and never skip a step.
- Be short, specific and confident. Cut "it should be noted that", "in order to", "at this point in time", "utilize" and "leverage".
- What you write may be the only impression you make. Pompous or fuzzy writing reads as a pompous or fuzzy mind.

## Utility script

`scripts/clutter_check.py` flags likely clutter in a draft, in the spirit of the brackets. Run it, then judge each flag yourself. It's a prompt for attention, not a verdict:

```bash
python3 scripts/clutter_check.py draft.md                    # human-readable report
python3 scripts/clutter_check.py draft.md --json             # machine-readable
cat draft.txt | python3 scripts/clutter_check.py -           # stdin
python3 scripts/clutter_check.py draft.md --only qualifier,inflated
python3 scripts/clutter_check.py draft.md --ignore passive,long-sentence
```

It flags inflated phrases, long words with short equivalents, verbs with unneeded prepositions, qualifiers, redundant adverbs, throat-clearing, fad words and jargon, journalese, clichés, travelese, pre-labeled facts, likely passives, concept-noun density, weak "there is" openers, misplaced "however", exclamation points, ambiguous contractions, "he/she", summary signals, common usage errors, long sentences (over 35 words) and long paragraphs (over 150 words). It skips YAML front matter, fenced code, inline code and URLs. It uses only the standard library.

For calibration: *On Writing Well* itself scores about 1.3 phrase flags per 100 words. A cluttered first draft often scores 5 or more. Don't chase zero. Judge each flag, and leave deliberate choices (a wry "too", a needed passive, a repetition for rhythm) alone.
